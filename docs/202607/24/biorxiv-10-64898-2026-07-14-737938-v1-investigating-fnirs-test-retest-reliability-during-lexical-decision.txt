Abstract 7

Significance. fNIRS is highly suitable for the study of reading development, however, the 8

reliability of its signals is not well understood during reading tasks. 9

Aim. Therefore, this study assessed the test -retest reliability of the fNIRS signal during a 10 

common event -related reading paradigm. 11 

Approach. English -speaking adults (n = 30) completed a lexical decision task during fNIRS 12 

recording twice, one week apart. 13 

Results. Our results demonstrated contrast effects partially consistent with prior neuroimaging 14 

literature, insofar as for each contrast, at least one predicted region of interest was activated. 15 

However, we did not identify significant activation in all predicted brain areas. Regarding group 16 

level test -retest reliability, we observed poor reliability across predicted brain regions for most 17 

conditions, with the exception of fair test -retest reliability in the left posterior temporal lobe for 18 

coarse lexical tuning. At the single -subject level, test -retest reliability ranged from poor to 19 

excellent across subjects, but was poor for most subjects. 20 

Conclusion. These results suggest fNIRS can detect changes in brain activation during a fast 21 

event -related reading task at the group level. However, reliability does not appear sufficient to 22 

interpret individual -level data. Further research should explore reliability across a wider range of 23 

designs to assess the generalizability of these findings. 24 

Keywords: Functional Near -Infrared Spectroscopy, Test -Retest Reliability, Reading, Lexical 25

Introduction 29 

Functional near infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) is a non -invasive, functional 30 

neuroimaging technique with adequate spatial and temporal resolution for cognitive neuroscience 31 

research .1–3 fNIRS has many benefits relative to other non -invasive neuro im aging techniques ,32 

such as being relatively insensitive to head motion, silent, and comfortable for the participant . 2,3 

33 

However, the reliability of the fNIRS signal requires further research. It is important to establish 34 

the reliability of fNIRS measurements at the group level to ensure that results are likely to be 35 

reproducible. Further, if fNIRS is to be used to investigate changes in brain activity 36 

longitudinally, or to relate individual differences in brain activity to behavioral abilities, it is 37 

necessary to confirm fNIRS provides a reliable snapshot of task -related brain activity at the level 38 

of individual participants. While fNIRS has been deemed reliable at the group level in select 39 

tasks (reviewed below) , its reliability has not been assessed with reading tasks . At the single 40 

subject level, reliability is also not yet well established and has often been reported as being 41 

low .1,4 

42 

Therefore, the first objective of the present study is to establish the test -retest reliability 43 

of the signals recorded by fNIRS during an event -related reading paradigm (lexical decision 44 

task) , at the group and single subject level. Additionally, as sufficient work has not yet been done 45 

to validate fNIRS in the study of reading ,5,6 the second objective i s to identify the brain areas that 46 

demonstrate significant activation for the effects of interest during the reading task , as measured 47 

by fNIRS, and identify whether these are consistent with results of prior research using fMRI. 48

Only a small subset of studies have investigated the reliability of the fNIRS signal at the 75 

subject level .4,16,18,20 While some participants demonstrate good to excellent test -retest reliability 76 

of task -related signals, others demonstrate minimal task -related activity, or task -related activity 77 

that differs from one session to the next , resulting in poor reliability .18,20 A number of factors 78 

have been identified , or hypothesized, to contribute to poor intra -individual reliability across 79 

fNIRS sessions. 80 

One suggested factor is p articipant state, such as affect or drowsiness, differing from one 81 

study visit to the next .9,16 Sleep deprivation and self -reported sleepiness are also associated with 82 

changes in the location and magnitude of the hemodynamic response in the brain .21,22 On the 83 

other hand, Huang et al. (2017) assessed participants’ affective states prior to each session and 84 

found no significant differences from one session to the next in participant affect, yet still 85 

observed poor subject -level reliability across sessions. Additionally, m oderate physical activity 86 

prior to neuroimaging results in both increases and decreases in cerebral blood perfusion, with 87 

the change in cerebral blood flow varying by brain region .23 Variability in the fNIRS signal at the 88 

subject level has also been suggested to vary with task type. For example, less -structured tasks 89 

may lead to more variable performance across repetitions of the task, and thus more variable task 90 

related brain activity from one session to the next .18 Hair colour and thickness may also 91 

contribute to differences in subject -level reliability between individuals , as both have been 92 

demonstrated to influence fNIRS signal quality .24,25 Additionally, it has been speculated that 93 

session -to -session variability in fNIRS optode placement on the scalp may reduce the reliability 94 

of the signal , though this variability has not been quantified .19

The Lexical Decision Taks and fNIRS 106 

The lexical decision task is a widely used experimental paradigm for studying the 107 

processes involved in single word reading .30 In a lexical decision task, participants read word or 108 

word -like stimuli and indicate if they are reading a real or non -real word. Performance during 109 

lexical decision relies on the engagement of orthographic, phonological, and semantic 110 

information 31 . While it has been used extensively in electroencephalography (EEG) and fMRI 111 

studies, it has received limited investigation with fNIRS (e.g., Hofmann et al., 2008; Sela et al., 112 

2014; Stela et al., 2011) , and no studies have examined its reliability in this context. 113 

Among the commonly obtained contrasts from lexical decision tasks, print tuning is an 114 

increased neural response to letter or text stimuli as compared to that for meaningless symbols; 115 

isolating orthographic processing. Using fMRI, print tuning has been associated with activation 116 

in the left parietal cortex, superior and inferior frontal gyri, fusiform gyrus, and inferior occipital -117 

temporal regions .34 –36

Sublexical tuning is the contrast between pronounceable non -words (“pseudowords”, 119 

e.g., “blorp ”) and unpronounceable patterns of letters (i.e., consonant strings), isolating processes 120 

involved in converting orthographic information to phonologic representations. fMRI studies of 121 

word reading have associated the left anterior superior temporal gyrus ,37 supramarginal gyrus ,38 

122 

inferior frontal gyrus ,39 –41 angular gyrus ,41 and inferior parietal lobule 39 with sublexical tuning. 123 

Lexical tuning is the contrast between real words and non -words. Lexical tuning contrasts 124 

can be described as “coarse” or “fine”, depending on whether the words are pronounceable or 125 

not. Coarse lexical tuning is the contrast between real words and non -pronounceable strings of 126 

letters (i.e., consonant strings) and fine lexical tuning is the contrast between real words and 127 

pseudowords. In fMRI studies, coarse lexical tuning is associated with increased activation in the 128 

visual word form area, left posterior temporoparietal cortex and left inferior temporal cortex ,42,43 

129 

while fine lexical tuning is associated with activation in the left angular, anterior superior 130 

temporal, and middle temporal gyri, and the left inferior and superior parietal lobule .44 –49 

131 

Across previous fNIRS studies using the lexical decision task, only fine lexical tuning has 132 

been investigated — in German ,33 Hebrew ,30,32 and Mandarin 50 — and results are inconsistent 133 

both between studies, and with prior fMRI work . Words have been reported to elicit greater 134 

activation than pseudowords in the left superior frontal and inferior parietal gyri ,33 and the left 135 

upper - and mid -frontal lobe ,30,32 while the third study reported activation only for pseudowords 136 

more than real words, in the left inferior frontal gyrus .50 Th e paucity of evidence from fNIRS, 137 

and the inconsistencies both between fNIRS studies and with prior fMRI results, underscores the 138 

need for further validation of the lexical decision task using fNIRS, and investigation of the 139 

reliabi lity of the results at the individual and group levels .140

The Present Study 141 

In the present study, we assessed the test -retest reliability of the neural activity for print 142 

tuning, sublexical tuning, coarse and fine lexical tuning contrasts as measured by fNIRS, at both 143 

the subject and group levels. We recruited adults to complete two identical study visits, one week 144 

apart , and had participants report on variables that may affect the fNIRS signal at the outset of 145 

each session. 146 

The first objective of this study was to assess the test -retest reliability of the fNIRS signal 147 

during lexical decision. Based on prior literature , we hypothesized the intraclass correlation 148 

(ICC) would show good (0.59 < ICC < 0.75) reliability at the group level ,4,9,16,18 with greater 149 

reliability when data was analyzed across clusters of channels (i.e., region of interest level) as 150 

compared to when data was analyzed at the individual channel level .4,9,18 Additionally, at the 151 

individual participant level, we hypothesized we would observe fair (0.40 < ICC < 0.59) 152 

reliability for the majority of participants .9,18 

153 

The second objective was to identify which brain regions demonstrate significant 154 

activation for the effects of interest as measured by fNIRS, and if these results align with prior 155 

fMRI literature. We hypothesized we would observe a significant print tuning effect (i.e., 156 

increased brain activity for consonant string as compared to false font stimuli) in the left inferior 157 

parietal lobe (LIPL) and inferior frontal gyrus (LIFG ). 34 –36,51 While a significant print tuning 158 

effect has also been observed in the left fusiform gyrus and left inferior occipito -temporal 159 

regions ,34 these brain areas cannot be reached by fNIRS due to the limited penetration depth of 160 

the near -infrared light .2 We also hypothesized a significant sublexical tuning effect (i.e., 161 

increased brain activity for pseudoword as compared to consonant string stimuli) would be 162 

observed in the left anterior temporal lobe (LATL), 37 LIFG , 39 –41 and LIPL .39 We hypothesized a 163

Lexical Decision Task Stimuli 200 

Four types of word stimuli were used in the lexical decision task: real words, 201 

pseudowords, consonant strings, and false fonts. The 60 real words were selected from a list of 202 

nouns that were six characters in length or less, from reading outcome word lists for the first, 203 

second, and third grade, to ensure they were readily recognizable. The 60 pseudowords were 204 

generated by a natural language model that combined high frequency pairs of letters from the 205 

English language, to form items matched in length to the list of real words, that follow legal 206 

letter patterns. The 60 consonant strings were developed by replacing the vowels in each real 207 

word with randomly selected consonants. Finally, the 60 false font stimuli were created by 208 

presenting the 60 pseudowords in the Brussels Artificial Character Sets (BACS) font .57

Behavioral Data 268 

The negative affect, positive affect, fatigue, and attentiveness index s cores on the 269 

PANAS -X56 were calculated and analyzed in Python (v3.12. 13 ), for each participant and study 270

Accuracy and reaction time for the lexical decision task were analyzed using the lme4 276 

package 62 in R (v4.4.2). For accuracy, single -trial data (correct/incorrect) was analyzed using 277 

linear mixed effects with a binomial family . We tested four models with condition as a fixed 278 

effect, with and without session as a fixed effect, and with random effects for participants, 279 

stimulus item, and stimulus condition (i.e., word type ). The best model was selected on the basis 280

session, with random effects for participants, stimulus item, and stimulus condition. Similarly, 285 

the best model was selected using AIC weights ,63 and included fixed effects for condition and 286

fNIRS Data 288 

Preprocessing. fNIRS data was preprocessed using the MNE (v1.9.0 )65 and MNE -NIRS 289 

(v0.7.1 )65,66 software packages in Python (v3.12.8). First, raw intensity values were converted to 290 

optical density. Then, optical density data was assessed for channels with poor scalp coupling, 291 

and channels with a scalp coupling index below 0.7 were marked as ‘bad’ channels. Next, 292 

temporal derivative distribution repair was used to identify and remove motion artifacts, 293 

(baseline shift and spike artifacts ). Short channel regression was then performed to reduce the 294 

influence of physiological signals on the data. Next, optical density data was converted to 295 

hemoglobin concentration using the modified Beer -Lambert law, and a negative correlation 296 

enhancement algorithm was used to further reduce the influence of motion artifacts. The data 297 

was then low pass filtered with a cutoff of 0.5 to remove physiological (cardiac and respiratory) 298 

artifacts . Lastly, channels marked as ‘bad’ were interpolated, such that data at the marked 299 

channels was recreated by interpolating the signal from nearby acceptable channels. 300 

First Level Analysis. For each experimental run, a design matrix was created to model 301 

the expected neural response to the presentation of each stimulus, convolved with a gamma 302 

function .67 Additional regressors were included in each design matrix to account for known 303 

sources of variance, including the temporal derivative of each condition’s gamma functions; the 304 

8 fNIRS short channels ; the six largest principal components derived from the nine raw 305 

accelerometer values, and a series of cosine basis functions up to 0.01 Hz to model low 306 

frequency artifacts. This design matrix was used to fit a general linear model to the preprocessed 307 

data using the MNE -NIRS library .308

for each run were imported into R (v . 4.4.2) and two data cleaning steps were performed. First, 310

Discussion 648 

The present study assessed the test -retest reliability of the fNIRS signal during a lexical 649 

decision task in the left and right hemispheres of the inferior frontal, superior temporal, and 650 

inferior parietal cortices. We analyzed the location, magnitude, and reliability of the fNIRS signal 651 

for common lexical decision effects. In general, when we collapsed data across sessions, each 652 

contrast of interest yielded significant activation (as indexed by HbO) in at least one predicted 653 

ROI, and activation in these ROIs was largely replicated when session date was analyzed 654 

separately. At the same time, however, at least one predicted ROI for each contrast did not show 655 

significant activation across or within sessions . As well, test -retest reliability across most regions 656 

and contrasts was poor at the group level, although it ranged from poor to excellent within 657 

individual participants. Most of our behavioral predictors, including affect, sleep, and caffeine 658 

consumption did not differ significantly between sessions , nor did any show a statistical 659 

relationship with ICC values, suggesting these variables were not responsible for the generally 660 

poor test -retest reliability of the measurements. Below we consider these findings in more detail. 661

We hypothesized we would observe test -retest reliability in the range of good (0.59 < 734 

ICC < 0.75) at the group level. However, w e observed consistently poor test -retest reliability 735 

when analyzing group data in the hypothesized ROIs for each contrast and in brain areas that 736 

demonstrated significant task -related activation. The low ICC values are particularly striking for 737 

brain regions that demonstrated significant changes in HbO and HbR both across sessions and in 738 

session 1 and 2 separately. That is, the activation was consistent from one session to the next, but 739 

the ICC results for those regions were still poor .740 

When analyzing group level test -retest reliability at the channel level, we identified some 741 

channels that demonstrated fair to excellent test -retest reliability. However, the majority of these 742 

sampled from brain regions not predicted (or demonstrated) to show significant effects; this may 743 

simply reflect channels that reliably showed a lack of task -related changes .744 

Additionally , we had hypothesized we would observe fair test -retest reliability across 745 

participants at the subject level, but on average subjects demonstrated poor test -retest reliability 746 

in brain regions predicted to demonstrate significant effects . While test -retest reliability of the 747 

fNIRS signal ranged from poor to excellent across subjects in predicted brain regions , most 748 

subjects ( >50%) demonstrated poor test -retest reliability across contrasts , consistent with the 749 

average . These results are somewhat in line with existing literature, as p rior investigations of the 750 

test -retest reliability of the fNIRS signal have documented high variability in subject level 751 

reliability, and task -related activation at the subject level .4,9,18 

752 

Overall, test -retest reliability at the group and subject level was lower than predicted, and 753 

group level results were lower than what has been reported in prior literature. While existing 754 

studies of the test -retest reliability of fNIRS at the group level have similarly reported varying 755

the measured hemodynamic response (e.g., block design) and that investigate more robust 770 

contrasts (e.g., stimulation compared to baseline). 771

Future Directions 795 

The present study aimed to identify the test -retest reliability of the fNIRS signal during a 796 

lexical decision task, to support future use of fNIRS to investigate individual differences in brain 797 

activation and how that relates to behavioral measures of reading skill. Considering the high 798 

level of variability in both task -related activation and test -retest reliability identified at the 799 

individual level, the results of this study suggest fNIRS may not be suitable for investigating 800 

individual differences in brain activation during a fast event -related lexical decision task. Further 801

Conclusions 808 

Overall, the present findings provide evidence suggesting fNIRS is adequately sensitive 809 

to detect reading -related brain activity during lexical decision at the group level . Moreover, the 810 

present study demonstrated that the ROIs showing significant task -related brain activity 811 

measured by fNIRS largely replicated across sessions at the group level, despite poor test -retest 812 

reliability values . Notably, it is relevant to consider the sensitivity of fNIRS to task -related brain 813 

activity may be limited by the magnitude of the contrast being investigated , as well as the 814 

coverage and consistency of coverage of specific brain regions across participants. Ultimately, to 815 

continue advancing the use of fNIRS in the study of reading, the field will need to develop 816 

protocols for ensuring precision and consistency of optode placement both across participants 817 

and measurement instances. 818
