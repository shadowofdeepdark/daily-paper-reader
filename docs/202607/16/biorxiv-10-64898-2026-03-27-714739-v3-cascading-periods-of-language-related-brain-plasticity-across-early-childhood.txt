Research highlights .24 

• Language has multiple, cascading sensitive periods, but the neural basis of these sensitive 25 

periods is not well -understood. 26 

• We leverage advances in in -vivo neuroimaging to quantify plasticity in language -related 27 

brain regions across childhood via Hurst, a measure of inhibition. 28 

• We find that Hurst increases (plasticity decreases) in a graded fashion, with posterior 29 

regions maturing before anterior regions. 30 

• Thalamic hurst plateaus in the first year of life, while cortical Hurst plateaus at age 9, 31

Abstract . Language is thought to have multiple sensitive periods in early childhood , but the neural 34 

basis of these sensitive periods is less understood . We leverage advances in in -vivo neuroimaging 35 

of plasticity , measuring the neural inhibition across the brain via Hurst exponent. Using two large 36 

datasets with children ages 10 months to 1 8 years (Baby Connectome Project: 10m -3y 6m , 458 37 

observations across n=222 children ; Human Connectome Project -Development: 5 -18y, n=437 ),38 

we characterize the development of the Hurst exponent in language -related brain regions. In early 39 

childhood, Hurst increases in temporal and frontal language areas , and posterior regions develop 40 

earlier than anterior regions . In contrast, thalamic Hurst plateaus earlier, perhaps underlying the 41 

earliest language -related sensitive periods. Children with higher language -related skills show 42 

slower increases in cortical Hurst in early childhood, suggesting protracted plasticity. Later in 43 

childhood , cortical Hurst plateaus around age 9, suggesting a potential neural mechanism for age -44 

related declines syntax learning. These results highlight a potential neural basis for cascading 45 

language -related sensitive periods. 46

Introduction 47 

Learning a new language is difficult for adults, but remarkably easy for children. This has 48 

long been explained by sensitive periods in language development: the idea that there are moments 49 

in development when children’s brains are particularly receptive to linguistic information, after 50 

which plasticity diminishes .1 Behaviorally, there is now ample evidence that there is not just one 51 

language -sensitive period, but a cascading set of sensitive periods for different language -related 52 

skills (e.g., phonetic perception, word pronunciation, syntax). 1 However , our understanding of the 53 

neural mechanisms underlying these sensitive periods is still sparse. Understanding the neural 54 

basis for sensitive periods in language development has the potential to provide foundational 55 

insights into basic questions about the neural basis of language as well as applied questions about 56 

when and how it becomes harder to learn a new language .57 

For hearing children, learning to distinguish speech sounds (phoneme perception) scaffolds 58 

later semantic and syntactic learning , each of which may have its own sensitive period . Phoneme 59 

perception is perhaps the best -characterized example of a sensitive period in language 60 

development, with children losing the ability to hear speech sounds not used in their native 61 

language between 6 -12 months of age. 2–4 For example, Hindi distinguishes between ṭa and ta , a 62 

contrast that non -Hindi speakers lose the ability to hear . Similarly , English distinguishes between 63 

ra and la , which Japanese does not use, and non -English -speaking Japanese speakers lose the 64 

ability to hear .2,5 Experience hearing a language in infancy seems to be sufficient to retain 65 

sensitivity to that language’s phonetic distinctions , even in the absence of later exposure .6,7 66 

Later in infancy , children show a rapid increase in word comprehension, followed by a 67 

stereotyped spurt in word production between ages 18 -24 months. Next , they develop language -68 

specific syntactic rules, which are another aspect of language thought to be governed by sensitive 69

At the neural level, language relies on a distributed set of regions across left superior 77 

temporal and frontal cortex , spanning lower -order sensor imotor cortex to higher -order association 78 

cortex. 12,13 The neural system for language appears to be in place by early childhood, though the 79 

language system becomes increasingly sensitive to language across this period. 14 The specific role 80 

of distinct language -related brain regions is contested. Classic accounts suggest temporal regions 81 

are more involved in comprehension, while frontal regions are more involved in production. 15 In 82 

support of this possibility, early studies found that people with damage to left superior temporal 83 

areas (Wernicke’s area) are more likely to show deficits in comprehension, while those with left 84 

inferior frontal areas show deficits in production. 15 However, large -scale evidence suggests that at 85 

least in adulthood, all of these regions work in concert to perform various language tasks. 16 –19 86 

Whether the same is true in childhood , as children are acquiring language , remains less clear. 87 

The t halamic nuclei also play a central role in the language system. Left thalamic nuclei 88 

are consistently activated along with frontal and temporal language areas in fMRI studies. 13,20 –22 89 

Supporting its causal role, deep -brain stimulation of thalamic nuclei in adult neurosurgery patients 90 

affects both semantic and syntactic performance. 23 The medial geniculate nucleus (MGN) is 91 

connected to the primary auditory cortex and is thought to play a role in the early gating of sound 92

Despite decades of work characterizing both milestones in language development and the 97 

neural basis of language, the neural mechanism underlying language -related sensitive periods 98 

remains unclear. One possibility is that the hierarchical development of the brain may underlie the 99 

cascading sensitive periods seen behaviorally. For example, sensor imotor regions of the brain 100 

develop earlier than higher -order association areas. 31 A developmental precursor to the 101 

sensorimotor -association ( S-A) axis may be the anterior -posterior axis 32 , in which posterior 102 

regions (toward the back of the brain) develop earlier than anterior regions (toward the front). 33,34 103 

Earlier -developing posterior temporal regions may align with the timing of earlier -developing 104 

language milestones (e.g., phoneme perception), while later -developing frontal regions may align 105 

with the timing of later language processes (e.g., production, syntax). 106 

One of the reasons we have not clearly identified the neural basis of language -related 107 

sensitive periods is that brain plasticity in young children has been remarkably hard to measure in 108 

vivo . Recently, advances in technology have allowed us to infer neural inhibition —which underlies 109 

plasticity —via fMRI with young children .35 –37 It is well established that increases in neural 110 

inhibition —particularly through the maturation of parvalbumin (PV) neurons —lead to reduced 111 

plasticity and the closure of sensitive periods. 38 Therefore, tracking the development of inhibition 112 

allows us to quantify both the peak and the subsequent reduction of plasticity that define these 113 

critical developmental windows. One strong contender for measuring inhibition in vivo in humans 114 

is the Hurst exponent, which quantifies long -range temporal correlations and scale -invariant 115

Here, we use d the Hurst exponent of fMRI data to examine the ages at which plasticity 125 

diminishes across language -related brain regions. We predicted that superior temporal regions 126 

would show a plateau in inhibition in the first year of life, in line with the timing of infants’ 127 

declining sensitivity to non -native phonetic categories. We posited that this plateau in inhibition 128 

might occur later in inferior frontal regions. We also predicted there would be cascading periods 129 

of inhibition across the sensor imotor -association gradient in language -related brain regions. 130 

Finally, we were interested in whether the trajectories of inhibitory development would be 131 

different depending on children’s early language skills. There is mixed evidence on whether brain 132 

development would be delayed as a result of more enriched input or alternatively sped up 31,43 –45 ,133 

therefore , we considered both possibilities .134

Hurst exponent 226 

For this study, we employed the fractional integration method to ensure robust and stable 227 

estimation. fractional integration models treat the time series as fractionally integrated processes, 228 

where the Hurst exponent H is defined as H = d + 0.5, with d representing the fractional integration 229 

order 42,54,55 . In these models, the autocorrelation function (ACF) decays according to a power law, 230 

approximately as ACF( k) ∼ k2d−1 , where k is the lag. This means that for d > 0, the correlations 231 

between distant observations decline slowly, reflecting long -range dependence or persistence in 232 

the signal. This approach fits parametric models to capture long -range dependencies more 233 

rigorously. In this study, the Hurst exponent was computed using the nonfractal MATLAB toolbox 234 

(https://github.com/wonsang/nonfractal )42,54 . The specific function utilized is bfn_mfin_ml.m 235 

function with the ‘filter’ argument set to ‘haar’ and the ‘ub ’ and ‘lb’ arguments set to [1.5,10] and 236 

[−0.5,0], respectively. This toolbox uses a discrete wavelet transform and a model of the time 237 

series as fractionally integrated processes and is estimated using maximum likelihood estimation.. 238

Global cortical hierarchies 239 

Because the secondary functional cortical hierarchy in infants has been shown to be an anterior -240 

posterior gradient 33 , we followed the methods of Lariviere and colleagues 33 to calculate the A -P241 

gradient using the BCP dataset. First, we calculated pairwise correlations between all 400 cortical 242 

parcels to construct a functional connectome. The resulting correlation matrix was z -transformed 243 

and averaged to produce a group -level connectome. We retained only the top 10% of connections 244 

and transformed the thresholded connectivity matrix into a normalized angle matrix, which scales 245 

the angle between vertex pairs according to their similarity. We then applied diffusion map 246 

embedding 56,57 . The resulting gradient closely matched the map published by Lariviere and 247 

colleagues 33 .248 

The sensorimotor -association (S -A) axis was derived by Sydnor and colleagues 48 .58 This 249 

map encompasses various cortical hierarchies, including functional connectivity gradients, 250 

evolutionary cortical expansion patterns, anatomical ratios, allometric scaling, brain metabolism 251 

measures, perfusion indices, gene expression patterns, primary modes of brain function, 252 

cytoarchitectural similarity gradients, and cortical thickness. 253 

To assess the relationship between each cortical gradient and age -related effects on the 254 

Hurst exponent, we used Spearman’s rank correlation and evaluated statistical significance using 255 

spin -based spatial permutation tests 59,60 , which preserve the spatial covariance structure typical in 256 

neuroimaging data. A null distribution was generated through 10,000 spherical rotations, against 257 

which the observed correlation was compared. 258

Statistical models 276 

To flexibly model both linear and nonlinear relationships between the Hurst exponent and age, we 277 

used Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) implemented via the mgcv package in R. In these 278 

models, the region -averaged Hurst exponent served as the dependent variable, age was included 279 

as a smooth term using a penalized regression spline with a maximum basis dimension of k = 3, 280 

and sex and mean framewise displacement were included as linear covariates. For the BCP dataset, 281 

we also controlled for site effects. Additionally, a random intercept for participant ID was included 282 

to account for within -subject variability. This factor -smooth interaction models subject -specific 283 

deviations from the population -level smooth, thereby accommodating the longitudinal structure of 284

Discussion 392 

Here, we explore d the neural basis for sensitive periods in language development. We used 393 

the Hurst exponent to characterize neural inhibition , a key modulator of neuroplasticity, in 394 

language -related brain regions from infancy to late childhood. We found that inhibition increased 395 

in temporal and frontal language -related cortex across early childhood. This increase in inhibition 396 

was not uniform across language cortex, but rather graded along cortical hierarchies . In contrast 397 

to the pattern in cortex, Hurst in thalamic nuclei associated with auditory processing and motor 398 

control plateaued within the first three years of life. This more closely aligns with the timing of 399 

the earliest language -related sensitive periods, including declines in non -native phoneme 400 

sensitivity . Thus, our data raise questions about whether this initial sensitive period is instantiated 401 

in thalamus, rather than cortex. In contrast, we found that cortical Hurst ultimately plateaued 402 

between ages 8 -10, paralleling evidence for a potential sensitive period in syntax learning around 403 

this age. Finally, we found some evidence that young children with higher early vocabularies had 404 

later -occurring increases in inhibition in language -related cortex. This suggests there may be 405 

prolonged plasticity for those with the highest language -related skills. 406 

We found that Hurst increased across temporal and frontal language -related cortex in the 407 

first few years of life. This pattern of data suggests that language -related sensitive periods 408 

instantiated in the cortex remain open past the first few years of life , contrary to our initial 409 

predictions . Thus, the earliest language -related sensitive periods in language do not appear to be 410 

instantiated in cortex. The trajectories of Hurst in temporal language regions followed similar 411 

developmental patterns as children’s developing comprehension skills, while trajectories of Hurst 412 

in frontal language regions more closely paralleled children’s developing production skills. 413

However, more research is needed to point to a mechanistic link between Hurst development and 414 

children’s developing language -related skills .415 

We also found that the maturation of Hurst in temporal and frontal language -related cortex 416 

was not uniform, but rather graded across well -known hierarchies of brain development. At the 417 

youngest ages, Hurst increased along the anterior -posterior axis, such that inhibition began to 418 

increase later for more anterior regions. Thus, in general, the more posterior temporal areas 419 

matured faster than the more anterior frontal areas. This hierarchical gradient may be a precursor 420 

to the sensorimotor -association (S -A) axis, which shows graded development across later 421 

childhood. 31,33,34 Indeed, among older children in our sample, the S -A axis better explained 422 

gradations in Hurst. This natural hierarchy may underly the graded nature of the development of 423 

language -related skills 1,31 . Language comprehension emerges earlier than production; in our 424 

sample, the time course of these developing skills visually mapped onto the development of 425 

inhibition for temporal and frontal language -related cortex, respectively. 426 

Our results suggest that language -related thalamic nuclei showed earlier maturation than 427 

cortex. Indeed, the thalamic nuclei we examined showed patterns of Hurst that paralleled the 428 

timing of early language -related sensitive periods, with inhibition plateauing in the first year of 429 

life. The medial geniculate nucleus (MGN) has connections to the auditory cortex and has been 430 

shown to play a role in processing phoneme distinctions, 24 –28 and appeared to have already reached 431 

a plateau in Hurst by age 10 months. This adds to growing evidence for the possibility that the 432 

sensitive period for phonemic discrimination —the ability to hear non -native speech sounds —may 433 

result from maturation of thalamus rather than cortex. Indeed, longstanding work points to the role 434 

of the MGN in processing speech sounds and gating information to the cortex, leading to 435 

arguments that the thalamus gates the discrimination of non -native speech phonemes 28,65 .436

We found that on average , Hurst in language -related cortex plateaued between ages 8 -10 437 

years old . The timing of this plateau aligns with longstanding evidence that a second language 438 

becomes harder to learn after these ages. 8,9,66 More recently, scholars have argued that the sensitive 439 

period for learning grammar in a new language extends until 18 years, and that prior findings had 440 

to do with years of exposure. 10 While we don’t have behavioral data to bear on this question, the 441 

timing of the plateau in Hurst in our sample aligns best with the former suggestion. These declines 442 

in plasticity may provide some neural evidence as to why there are declines in second language 443 

learning around age 9-10 years .444 

In terms of individual differences, we found that higher vocabulary was linked to protracted 445 

development of temporal language regions. Between 10 months and 3 .5 years, we found a 446 

significant interaction, such that children with lower vocabulary comprehension and production 447 

had steeper, more positive slopes of Hurst, suggesting that plasticity was declining more rapidly. 448 

Given that children with earlier phonetic commitment to their native language show more rapid 449 

vocabulary growth, one possibility we considered was that higher vocabulary would be associated 450 

with earlier sensitive periods. Indeed, some theories have suggested that plasticity may close when 451 

a child has received sufficient input to commit to a language, independent of other factors. 67 452 

However, our findings argue against this possibility. Because children with the highest 453 

vocabularies in infancy are those who receive the most linguistic input 68 , our findings are more in 454 

line with theories that richer, more dynamic input may lead to protracted sensitive periods. 1 This 455 

more dynamic input extending sensitive periods may also help to explain why children learning 456 

multiple languages may show more protracted sensitive periods. 67,69 It may also help explain recent 457 

findings that young children with high phonological processing skills show more long -lasting 458 

increases in the volume and surface area of several language -related brain regions. 70 Of course, it 459

Our work raises questions about whether protracted periods of sensitivity are due to 462 

language input or other variables such as stress or other features of the child’s environment. In our 463 

study, the results h eld when controlling for children’s family socioeconomic status . Children’s 464 

language input is related to their language learning above and beyond SES 71 , whereas effects of 465 

stressful life events appear to be mediated through language input 72 ; thus, we believe our findings 466 

point to a more direct role for a child’s language input. Still, it is possible that children whose 467 

vocabularies were lower also experienced more stressful life events, which sped up their neural 468 

development. 43 The children in our samples also came from relatively high -socioeconomic 469 

backgrounds. In the BCP dataset, nearly three -quarters of caregivers had a college degree or 470 

higher, while in the United States in 20 22, about 3 7.7% of adults age 25 and over had a college 471 

degree or higher 61 .73 In the HCPD dataset , in which parental education was not available , 60% of 472 

families had incomes above $100K/year , while in the United States in 2022 , approximately 37.8 %473 

of families had incomes above $100k .74 Thus, future work should examine plasticity in language -474 

related brain regions for children from a more diverse range of backgrounds and measure 475 

children’s stress and language input directly. 476 

Several additional potential limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, our 477 

measures of children’s language development were limited. We only had parent -reported measures 478 

of infants’ word knowledge as opposed to direct assessments, and no tests that measured children’s 479 

phoneme distinction. Moreover , the BCP collects brain imaging and vocabulary measures at 480 

different timepoints, which limits the temporal precision with which neural and behavioral data 481 

can be linked. Second , in the BCP dataset, infants under 3 years old were sleeping during the scan ,482

In sum, this work elucidat ed trajectories of inhibitory development for language -related 492 

brain regions across cortex and thalamus . Our findings point to graded periods of plasticity across 493 

language -related brain regions , perhaps driven by global cortical hierarchies . It is possible that 494 

these cortical hierarchies underlie the graded nature of language -related sensitive periods that have 495 

been observed behaviorally , such that, for example, language perception skills develop earlier than 496 

language production skills . Our data also add to growing evidence suggesting the earliest -observed 497 

language -related sensitive periods may be instantiated in thalamus, rather than cortex, though more 498 

work is needed to measure this directly. Ultimately, understanding these sensitive periods may be 499 

useful for time -sensitive language -related interventions, as well as for our basic understanding of 500 

the basis of the human language system. 501
