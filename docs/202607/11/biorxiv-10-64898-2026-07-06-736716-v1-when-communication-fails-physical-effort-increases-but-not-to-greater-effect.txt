Abstract 17 

Successful communication depends not just on what we say but also on how we repair it 18 

when understanding breaks down. People routinely respond to misunderstanding by 19 

gesturing more or speaking louder. Yet whether these responses actually reflect 20 

measurable bodily effort, and whether it helps communication, has not yet been directly 21 

tested. Extending the hypo - and hyper -articulation accounts to whole -body multimodal 22 

communication we ask: does regaining understanding require increased physical effort, 23 

and does that effort improve communicative outcomes? In a pre -registered biomechanical 24 

study, 61 pairs of participants conveyed concepts without conventionalized language, 25 

using gestures, vocalizations, or both, and repaired failed attempts. We measured 26

communicative effort by capturing arm torque change (upper -limb effort), vocal amplitude 27 

(vocal effort), and postural adjustments (postural effort). Using Bayesian hierarchical 28 

models, we show that repair increases physical effort across all three measurements, but 29 

that performers communicate more over time rather than more forcefully at any particular 30 

moment in time. Upper -limb effort scale with the degree of misunderstanding but effort 31 

overall does not predict whether communication succeeds. We suggest that 32 

communicative effort on the level we measured here functions as an index of continued 33 

commitment rather than a mechanism for repairing misunderstanding. 34 

# Main 35 

Communication is like a dance; it takes two partners, requires continuous effort, and when 36 

one stumbles, the other may work harder to keep going. When communication fails, 37 

partners may not only repeat themselves, but also speak louder or gesture more 38 

extensively, investing more physical effort to sustain the exchange. Yet although this bodily 39 

investment is central to everyday multimodal communication, its physical dimension 40 

remains largely unexplored. The current pre -registered study investigates whether 41 

communicative modulations people make in response to misunderstanding are associated 42 

with a measurable increase in physical effort and what that cost reveals about the body’s 43 

role in keeping communication alive. 44

continuous negotiation of metabolic, computational, cognitive, and biomechanical 46 

constraints ( Bernstein, 1967 ; Ferrer -i-Cancho et al., 2013 ; Kelso, 1995 ; Kool et al., 2010 ;47 

McAllister et al., 2025 ; Rosen, 1967 ; Tversky & Kahneman, 1974 ; Wong et al., 2021 ). This is 48

reflects effort, particularly the optimization toward low -cost solutions ( Gibson et al., 2019 ;50

operationalized primarily in terms of message length and information structure. Short, 52 

frequent forms require less articulation and place fewer demands on processing ( Zipf, 53 

1949 ), and the principle of effort optimization has been argued to shape lexical frequency, 54 

word order, syntactic structure, sound duration, and articulatory detail across languages 55 

(Aylett & Turk, 2004 ; Bell et al., 2009 ; Futrell et al., 2015 ; Haspelmath, 2021 ). These 56 

regularities, often explained in information -theoretic terms, may themselves have a 57 

physical basis ( Torre et al., 2019 ). In speech sciences, physical effort has been used to 58 

explain why vocal targets are often not fully realized, resulting in phonetic reductions 59 

(Kirchner, 2001 ; Lindblom, 1990 ). What these traditions have in common is that they have 60 

focused almost exclusively on the structure and production of spoken language, with a few 61 

exceptions extending these accounts to sign ( Napoli et al., 2014 ; Slonimska et al., 2022 ). 62

emerges from movement shaped by the constraints of human physiology ( Kadavá et al., 66

biomechanics of the tongue and mandible ( MacNeilage & Davis, 2000 ); head and arm 68 

gestures involve substantial mass that interacts with body systems crucial for vocalization, 69 

such as the larynx ( Ćwiek & Fuchs, 2025 ) and the lungs ( Pouw et al., 2020 ); and the forces 70

In the current study, we test whether the modulations that people make in their 110 

communicative behavior to regain understanding are associated with an increase in 111 

physical effort. We define physical effort as an energetic cost of an action ( Halperin & 112 

Vigotsky, 2024 ) associated with the enhancement of (a feature of) an articulator ( Lindblom, 113 

1990 ), and treat increased effortfulness as a shift from a low -cost baseline toward hyper -114

masses being moved and the dynamic demands of maintaining balance. 130

greater peak force (henceforth ‘instantaneous effort’)  – or by extending the action over a 133 

longer window without necessarily increasing its peak intensity (henceforth ‘cumulative 134 

effort’ ). If effort is concentrated in a single moment, peak intensity and total cost are 135 

essentially the same thing. But once effort is spread out across several moments or a 136

We test two pre -registered hypotheses (H1, H2). In our referential task design (Fig. 1a), 143 

participants convey concepts without conventionalized language, using gestures, 144 

vocalizations, or both. Each failed guess from their partner triggers an obligatory correction 145 

attempt. Based on prior research ( Lindblom, 1990 ), we expect these correction attempts to 146 

result in hyper -articulated behavior. Thus, we predict that  correction recruits more physical 147 

effort than baseline performance  (H1). Further, because interlocutors are known to adapt 148 

their multimodal signals depending on shared knowledge with their partners ( Gerwing & 149 

Bavelas, 2004 ; Hilliard & Cook, 2016 ; Holler & Wilkin, 2011 ; Levshina, 2022 ), we assume 150 

that the guesser’s answer provides the performer with possibility to assess the degree of 151 

common ground and this with the opportunity to act on it, taking into consideration how far 152 

this answer is from the target meaning (for instance, by enhancing communicative 153 

movements). Concretely, we predict that  a higher degree of misunderstanding recruits 154 

more physical effort from the performer  (H2). Because the communicative modality 155 

available for use in the different experimental conditions determines which articulators are 156 

available for elaboration, we additionally examine how effort levels and communicative 157

## Misunderstanding recruits cumulative but not instantaneous effort 169 

As predicted, participants invested more effort when their partners misunderstood them 170 

but the pattern was more specific than a simple mobilization of all available resources. 171

To summarize, while cumulative effort increased reliably across corrections for all three 237 

measures, instantaneous effort did not. Correction therefore imposes a clear cumulative 238 

cost on performers without recruiting greater peak intensity per movement or per 239 

vocalization. This pattern is consistent with a redistribution of effort across longer time 240 

intervals and more movement units rather than a concentrated scaling -up of force or 241 

amplitude, an interpretation we develop in the Discussion. The consistency of these 242 

increases across three independent measures of bodily effort supports that correction 243 

imposes a substantive physical cost on the performer, supporting H1 for cumulative, 244 

though not instantaneous, effort. 245

## Upper -limb effort scales with the degree of misunderstanding 266 

We found partial support for H2 (Fig. 2c). Cumulative upper -limb effort scaled credibly with 267 

the degree of misunderstanding, operationalized as the cosine similarity between the 268 

guesser’s previous answer and the target meaning (lower similarity indicates greater 269 

misunderstanding). Each 1 -SD increase in cosine similarity was associated with 7.5% 270 

reduction in cumulative arm torque change (95% CrI: [ -11.3%,  -3.5%]); equivalently, 271 

performers invested more cumulative upper -limb effort when the previous answer was 272 

further from the target. Instantaneous arm torque change ran in the same direction, but its 273 

estimated interval included zero (β =  -2.5%, 95% CrI: [ -5.4%, 0.5%]). In contrast, neither 274 

cumulative nor instantaneous vocal effort showed a credible relationship with cosine 275 

similarity, and the same was true for both postural measures. 276 

Cumulative upper -limb effort therefore adapts reliably to the amount of repair work the 277 

previous answer requires, while vocal and postural effort appear insensitive to the degree 278 

of misunderstanding. Importantly, the effect of semantic distance on cumulative upper -279 

limb effort was uniformly distributed across the sample: the random -slope variance for 280 

cosine similarity was negligible across all three grouping levels (concepts, participants, 281 

and dyads), indicating that the effect did not vary systematically by performer, dyad, or 282 

concept. 283

# Discussion 317 

When people fail to communicate a concept through non -linguistic means, they do not 318 

only reorganize their behavior  – they invest more physical effort. In the present study, they 319 

did so in a specific way: not by intensifying their actions, but by doing more over time. This 320 

distinction between intensification and elaboration may point toward a principle of how the 321 

body responds to misunderstanding in a communicative context under conditions where 322 

detailed linguistic repair is unavailable. Whether the elaboration consists of a qualitatively 323 

distinct motor strategy  – for instance, recruiting additional movement units or extending 324 

spatial trajectories  – or whether it emerges partly from the temporal structure of repetition 325 

itself remains an open question. What our data show is that the overall effort investment 326 

increased with misunderstanding, and that this increase unfolded over time rather than in 327 

the form of amplified bursts. The increase was also flexible  – sensitive to how large the 328 

misunderstanding was and shaped by the articulatory resources available. We thereby 329 

show that misunderstanding literally recruits investment of physical (and by proxy an 330

segment moves, and can be estimated by inverse dynamic analysis. To prevent 654 

amplification of noise when solving inverse dynamics, we first smoothed the joint -angle 655 

data using a Savitzky -Golay filter with a span of 560 ms and a polynomial order of 3. The 656 

average root mean square error was 0.03 m. 657

## Data analysis 706 

Confirmatory analysis 707 

Confirmatory analyses concern pre -registered analysis and hypotheses. All statistics were 708 

performed using  R (Team, 2025 ). We fitted Bayesian mixed effects models, using the  brms 709 

package ( Bürkner, 2017 ), to test two preregistered hypotheses: 710 

H1: Correction recruits more physical effort than the baseline performance. 711 

H2: A higher degree of misunderstanding will require a performer to engage in more effortful 712 

correction. 713 

For H1, we fitted six sets of models for the six investigated dependent variables: 1) arm 714 

torque integral (cumulative upper -limb effort), 2) envelope integral (cumulative vocal 715 

effort), 3) COPc integral (cumulative postural effort), 4) arm torque peak mean 716 

(instantenous upper -limb effort), 5) envelope peak mean (instantenous vocal effort), 6) 717 

COPc peak mean (instantenous postural effort). The primary predictor of interest was 718 

correction. To identify the most appropriate model specification, we fitted four variants for 719 

each dependent variable, increasing in complexity. 720 

Model 0 included only the primary predictor as a fixed effect. 721 

Model 1 additionally included covariates: familiarity between the guesser and the 722 

performer, extraversion of the performer (BFI), expressibility of the concept, modality, and 723 

trial number. These were included not as predictors of primary interest but as theoretically 724

motivated adjustment variables. To guide covariate selection, we constructed a directed 725 

acyclic graph (DAG) reported in full in the Supplementary Material (section E). The DAG 726 

formalized our assumptions about the causal structure of the data  – specifically, which 727 

variables influence both the likelihood of receiving a correction and the effort expended 728 

during it. A variable that affects both the treatment (correction) and the outcome (effort) 729 

without lying on the causal path between them constitutes a confounder; failing to adjust 730 

for it would bias estimates of the correction effect. In our DAG, familiarity, extraversion, 731 

expressibility, modality, and trial number all plausibly met this criterion: for example, more 732 

expressive concepts are easier to communicate and therefore less likely to require 733 

correction, while also requiring less physical effort. Leaving expressibility uncontrolled 734 

would therefore inflate the apparent effect of correction on effort. All fixed effect predictors 735 

were pre -registered before data analysis. Model 1 included random intercepts and slopes 736 

for all predictors that vary within each grouping factor (participants, dyads, concepts). 737 

Although dyad -level random effects were not formally pre -registered, we included them 738 

because participants were nested within dyads by design  – dyad partners shared the same 739 

interactional context, and ignoring this clustering would lead to underestimation of 740 

uncertainty in fixed -effects estimates. 741

intercepts and slopes for each grouping factor. 743

effects. This simplification was motivated empirically: in Model 2, most correlation 745
