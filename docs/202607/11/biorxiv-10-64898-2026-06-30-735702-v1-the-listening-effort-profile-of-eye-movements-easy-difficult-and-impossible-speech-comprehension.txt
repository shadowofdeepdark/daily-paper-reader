Abstract 33 

Speech comprehension in noisy environments often requires cognitive effort, but listeners may 34 

disengage when comprehension becomes impossible. Eye movements have recently emerged 35 

as a promising new measure of listening effort, but it remains unclear whether eye movements 36 

are sensitive to the full effort profile across easy, difficult, and impossible speech 37 

comprehension. Across four experiments, participants listened to sentences at easy, difficult, 38 

and impossible levels of multi-talker background babble while pupil size and eye movements 39 

were recorded. Pupil size generally followed the expected inverted u-shaped effort profile: low 40 

for easy speech, maximal for difficult but still intelligible speech and lower again for impossible 41 

speech, although this pattern partly reflected sustained, condition-specific differences and not 42 

only sentence-evoked responses. Gaze dispersion – measuring the spread of eye movements – 43 

decreased with high temporal selectivity during difficult relative to easy and impossible speech, 44 

indicating reduced eye movements during active, effortful listening. However, gaze dispersion 45 

was also lower, but less temporally selective, during impossible compared to easy listening, 46 

especially in non-baseline-corrected analyses, suggesting that reduced eye movements do not 47 

index listening effort uniquely. Instead, eye movements appear to reflect both attentional 48 

engagement during difficult listening and disengagement or inward attention when meaningful 49 

listening is no longer possible. These findings indicate that pupil size and eye movements 50 

provide complementary indices of listening-related cognition, and highlight the integration of 51 

listening, cognition, and motor systems. 52

Introduction 54 

Speech comprehension in everyday environments is often challenged by background sound, 55 

such as competing talkers in a restaurant or traffic noise on a busy street. Under such 56 

conditions, listeners must rely more strongly on cognitive resources, including selective 57 

attention, working memory, and prior knowledge, to extract meaning from the masked acoustic 58 

signal. This increased cognitive investment is commonly referred to as listening effort (Pichora-59 

Fuller et al., 2016; Peelle, 2018; Herrmann and Johnsrude, 2020). Assessing listening effort is 60 

important because people may experience speech comprehension as effortful even when 61 

performance remains relatively high (Kadem et al., 2020; Winn and Teece, 2021), and such effort 62 

may provide an early indication of hearing-related difficulties before overt communication 63 

failures become apparent (Pichora-Fuller et al., 2016; Helfer and Jesse, 2021). 64 

One of the most widely used objective measures of listening effort is pupillometry (Winn 65 

et al., 2018; Zekveld et al., 2018). Pupil size typically increases when speech comprehension 66 

becomes more demanding, for example when speech is masked by background noise or 67 

degraded acoustically (Zekveld et al., 2010; Zekveld and Kramer, 2014; Ohlenforst et al., 2017b; 68 

Ohlenforst et al., 2018; Wendt et al., 2018; Kadem et al., 2020; Neagu et al., 2023; Baldock et al., 69 

2024). Pupillometry has therefore provided an important physiological window into the cognitive 70 

demands of listening. However, pupil size is also sensitive to factors unrelated to listening effort, 71 

including luminance, gaze position relative to the eye-tracking camera, and arousal-related 72 

fluctuations (Brisson et al., 2013; Knapen et al., 2016; Mathôt, 2018; Fink et al., 2024). Moreover, 73 

recent work suggests that pupillometric effects may be less robust in more naturalistic, 74 

continuous speech-listening paradigms than in trial-based sentence-listening tasks (Cui and 75 

Herrmann, 2023; Herrmann et al., 2025; see also Widmann et al., 2025). 76 

A growing body of work suggests that eye movements may provide an alternative or 77 

complementary marker of listening effort. When speech listening becomes more difficult, 78 

listeners reduce their eye movements, as reflected in fewer micro-saccades and reduced spatial 79 

gaze dispersion (Contadini-Wright et al., 2023; Cui and Herrmann, 2023; Herrmann and Ryan, 80 

2024; Cui et al., 2025; He et al., 2025; Herrmann et al., 2025; Mariotti et al., 2026). These 81 

reductions have been observed across different speech materials, including isolated sentences 82 

and continuous stories, across different visual contexts, including blank screens, fixation, and 83 

moving-dot displays, and across stationary and mobile devices (Cui and Herrmann, 2023; 84 

Herrmann and Ryan, 2024; Cui et al., 2025; Herrmann et al., 2025). Mechanistically, reduced eye 85 

movements during effortful listening may help limit distracting visual input or may reflect 86 

broader interactions between oculomotor, attentional, and auditory systems. For example, 87 

periods with reduced eye movements have been associated with changes in auditory cortical 88 

excitability that could support sound processing (Schneider and Mooney, 2015, 2018; O'Connell 89 

et al., 2020). 90

A useful physiological measure of listening effort should be sensitive not only to 91 

increases in task demand, but also to disengagement. Listeners do not always continue 92 

investing effort as listening becomes more difficult. When the demands of a listening situation 93 

exceed the listener’s available resources, or when the expected reward no longer justifies the 94 

required cognitive investment, listeners may disengage or “give up” (Yerkes and Dodson, 1908; 95 

Brehm and Self, 1989; Richter, 2013; Westbrook and Braver, 2015; Heffernan et al., 2016; 96 

Pichora-Fuller et al., 2016; Herrmann and Johnsrude, 2020). Thus, effort measures should 97 

capture the full effort profile: low effort when speech is easy, increased effort when speech is 98 

difficult but still potentially understandable, and reduced effort again when comprehension 99 

becomes impossible. 100 

Pupillometry appears to follow this expected inverted u-shape effort profile in many 101 

listening studies. Pupil size is often largest when speech is difficult but still intelligible, and 102 

smaller when speech is either easy to understand or impossible to understand (Zekveld and 103 

Kramer, 2014; Ohlenforst et al., 2017a; Ohlenforst et al., 2018; Wendt et al., 2018; Herrmann 104 

and Ryan, 2024). Eye movements, however, may follow a different pattern. In previous work, 105 

gaze dispersion – a measure of the spread of a person's eye movements over a period of time – 106 

decreased when speech comprehension was difficult compared to easy, but also remained 107 

reduced when speech comprehension was impossible (Herrmann and Ryan, 2024). This 108 

sustained reduction was observed even after sentence offset, when acoustic stimulation no 109 

longer differed between easy, difficult, and impossible conditions, and even when participants 110 

could anticipate that an upcoming trial would be impossible. These findings suggest that pupil 111 

size and eye movements index different aspects of listening-related cognition (Herrmann and 112 

Ryan, 2024; Liu and Chait, 2026). 113 

Why eye movements remain low during impossible listening is unresolved. One 114 

possibility is that visual stimulation in previous work, such as a moving-dot display, encouraged 115 

listeners to suppress visual exploration, thereby producing reduced eye movements that were 116 

not specific to listening (Herrmann and Ryan, 2024). Another possibility is that listeners 117 

continued searching for a sentence in the background noise, even when comprehension was 118 

impossible. Because the sentence was inaudible, listeners may not have known when it ended 119 

and may have continued orienting attention to the auditory stream after the actual sentence 120 

offset. Under this account, eye movements should eventually increase if the post-sentence 121 

interval is long enough for listeners to infer that no more speech will occur. A third possibility is 122 

that reduced eye movements during impossible listening reflect a different state altogether, 123 

such as disengagement or mind wandering, since inward attention can reduce eye movements 124 

(Reichle et al., 2010; Benedek et al., 2017; Walcher et al., 2017; Mézière et al., 2026). If so, 125 

reduced eye movements might persist throughout a listening trial, even before or after the time 126 

window in which speech could be processed, especially when the visual scene provides little 127 

reason to explore. 128

The current study investigated these possibilities across four experiments by examining 129 

pupil size and gaze dispersion during easy, difficult, and impossible speech comprehension. In 130 

Experiment 1, we tested whether reduced gaze dispersion during impossible listening occurs in 131 

the absence of any visual stimulation, using a blank screen rather than a moving-dot display. In 132 

Experiment 2, we extended the post-sentence interval to determine whether reduced eye 133 

movements in the impossible condition would recover once listeners had more time to realize 134 

that the sentence had ended. In Experiment 3, we removed the sentence entirely from the 135 

impossible condition and informed participants that no sentence would be present, thereby 136 

further discouraging continued attempts to extract speech from the noise. In Experiment 4, we 137 

tested whether visually engaging stimulation would increase eye movements during periods of 138 

disengagement and reveal a more selective reduction during difficult speech processing. 139 

Together, these experiments provide a comprehensive assessment of how pupil size and eye 140 

movements track the transitions from easy listening, to effortful listening, to impossible or 141 

disengaged listening. 142 

General methods 143 

Participants 144 

Participants were native English speakers or individuals who learned English before the age of 5 145 

years. They reported having normal hearing and neither had a prescription for hearing aids or 146 

wore them. Participants gave written informed consent prior to the experiment and were paid 147 

$10 CAD per half-hour for their participation. The study was conducted in accordance with the 148 

Declaration of Helsinki, the Canadian Tri-Council Policy Statement on Ethical Conduct for 149 

Research Involving Humans (TCPS2-2014), and was approved by the Research Ethics Board of 150 

the Rotman Research Institute at Baycrest. Detailed demographic information about 151 

participants is provided in the individual experiment sections below. 152 

Experimental setup 153 

Participants sat in a single-wall sound booth (Eckel). Experimental procedures were run using 154 

Psychtoolbox (v3.0.14) in MATLAB (MathWorks Inc.) on a Lenovo T450s laptop with Microsoft 155 

Windows 7 (Experiments 1-3) or a desktop computer with Windows 10 (Experiment 4), all in the 156 

same space. The laptop/computer screen was mirrored to a ViewSonic monitor in the sound 157 

booth. Participants sat in front of the ViewSonic (60 Hz refresh rate) monitor at a distance of 158 

about 70 cm with their head stabilized through a chin and forehead rest. Sounds were presented 159 

using a RME Fireface UCX external sound card and Sony Dynamic Stereo MDR-7506 160 

headphones at a comfortable listening level of ~70 dB SPL. 161

Stimulus materials and procedure 162 

The stimuli comprised sentences selected from the Harvard sentence lists, spoken by a male 163 

native speaker of English (average duration: ~2.5 seconds) (IEEE, 1969). The audio recordings 164 

were obtained from a publicly accessible resource (speaker ID: ’PNM055’; 165 

https://depts.washington.edu/phonlab/projects/uwnu.php; Panfili et al., 2017; McCloy et al., 166 

2018). Each sentence was embedded within a 12-talker babble background noise (Bilger, 1984), 167 

with the sentence beginning 2.8 s after the babble onset (Experiment 1; similar to Herrmann and 168 

Ryan, 2024) or 1.5 s after babble onset (Experiments 2-4). The duration of the babble masker was 169 

6.3 s in Experiment 1 and 8.5 s in Experiments 2-4. All sentences terminated before the babble 170 

ended. Sentences were presented at three signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs): +11 dB (easy), -4 dB 171 

(difficult), and -19 dB (impossible). In Experiments 3 and 4, the impossible condition did not 172 

contain any sentence (see below). Listening effort was expected to be minimal at the extreme 173 

SNRs (easy and impossible), as participants either required little cognitive effort or were unable 174 

to understand the sentence and presumably would stop trying. In contrast, the highest effort 175 

was anticipated in the difficult, but still intelligible, SNR condition. Different SNRs were created 176 

by modifying the level of sentences while keeping the level of the babble noise constant across 177 

trials (Ohlenforst et al., 2017a; Kadem et al., 2020; Cui and Herrmann, 2023; Herrmann and 178 

Ryan, 2024). This ensured that stimuli with different SNRs did not differ prior to sentence onset 179 

and after sentence offset (Ohlenforst et al., 2017a; Kadem et al., 2020; Cui and Herrmann, 180 

2023). 181 

The computer screen was blank and mid-gray before and during sound stimulation 182 

(except in Experiment 4). Participants were free to look around the screen however they wished 183 

(but keep their gaze on the screen), while reducing blinks (Johansson et al., 2006; Johansson et 184 

al., 2011; Johansson et al., 2012; Cui and Herrmann, 2023; Herrmann and Ryan, 2024). A probe 185 

word appeared on the screen after the sound on each trial, and participants had to decide 186 

whether it was semantically related or unrelated to the preceding sentence with a keypress 187 

(Rodd et al., 2010a; Rodd et al., 2010b; Kadem et al., 2020; Cui and Herrmann, 2023). 188 

Participants were asked to guess if they did not know. 189 

Each SNR condition included 60 sentence trials, distributed across five experimental 190 

blocks. Within each block, participants listened to 12 sentences at each SNR. Trials were 191 

grouped by SNR, meaning 12 sentences with the same SNR were presented consecutively. This 192 

block-wise structure was intentional: by informing participants in advance of the upcoming 193 

listening difficulty (easy, difficult, or impossible), they could prepare for the difficulty level of the 194 

upcoming trials (Herrmann and Ryan, 2024). Had the SNR conditions been randomized on a 195 

trial-by-trial basis, participants would not have known when a sentence would be unintelligible, 196 

and thus might have attempted to exert effort even in the impossible condition – an outcome 197 

that would have confounded our investigation. 198

To prevent potential confounding of specific sentences with SNR conditions, the 199 

sentence-to-SNR mapping was randomized across participants. Prior to the main experiment, a 200 

brief training block of 12 trials was used to familiarize participants with the semantic-201 

relatedness task and the eye-tracking calibration process. 202 

Behavioral data analysis 203 

For each SNR condition, the proportion of correct responses in the semantic-relatedness task 204 

was calculated. Proportion data were analyzed using a repeated-measures analysis of variance 205 

(rmANOVA) with the within-participant factor SNR (+11 [easy], -4 [hard], -19 [impossible] dB). 206 

Post hoc tests with correction for multiple comparisons using Holm’s method (Holm, 1979) were 207 

calculated in the case of a significant SNR effect. 208 

Pupillometry and eye-movement recordings 209 

The pupil size and eye movements were concurrently recorded from the left and right eye using 210 

an EyeLink Duo eye-tracker (SR Research Ltd.) at a sampling rate of 500 Hz. In the beginning of 211 

each experimental block, a nine-point fixation was used to calibrate the eye-tracker (McIntire et 212 

al., 2014). 213 

Processing of pupil-size and eye-movement data 214 

Data analysis was conducted in MATLAB software (MathWorks Inc.). The main metrics were 215 

pupil size and gaze dispersion. Gaze dispersion is a measure of spread or variability of eye 216 

movements over time that non-specifically captures any eye movement changes associated 217 

with difficult listening (Cui and Herrmann, 2023; Herrmann and Ryan, 2024; Cui et al., 2025; 218 

Herrmann et al., 2025). Gaze dispersion behavior in response to listening challenge is similar to 219 

that of (micro-)saccade rate (Contadini-Wright et al., 2023; Herrmann et al., 2025). However, 220 

gaze dispersion is preferred here because it appears to be more sensitive to listening challenges 221 

than (micro-)saccade rate (Herrmann et al., 2025), likely in part because (micro-)saccade data 222 

are sparse (occurring ~1-2 per second), whereas gaze dispersion is based on the continuous 223 

time series data. 224 

Preprocessing was calculated separately for each block and separately for each eye. 225 

Missing data points in the pupil and x–y eye-coordinate time courses resulting from blinks or 226 

other artifacts were set to NaN (‘not a number’ in MATLAB), as were data points from 0.1 s before 227 

to 0.2 s after the artifact. Pupil-size values that differed from the mean pupil size by more than 3 228 

times the standard deviation in a block were classified as outliers. The corresponding pupil-size, 229 

x, and y data points were set to NaN. 230

For the pupil size, NaN-coded data points were interpolated using MATLAB’s ‘pchip’ 231 

method. Pupil-size time courses were filtered with a 5-Hz low-pass filter (51 points, Kaiser 232 

window; β = 4). Pupil-size time courses for the left and right eye were averaged. 233 

For gaze dispersion, NaN-coded points were ignored. Gaze dispersion was calculated as 234 

the standard deviation in gaze across time points, averaged across x- and y-coordinates, and 235 

transformed to logarithmic values to make the metric’s distributional properties more Gaussian. 236 

Smaller values indicate less gaze dispersion (i.e., fewer eye movements). To obtain time 237 

courses, gaze dispersion was calculated for 1-s (500-sample) sliding time windows centered 238 

sequentially on each time point. If more than 90% of data were unavailable within a 1-s time 239 

window (that is, 450 or more samples were NaN-coded), gaze dispersion for the corresponding 240 

time point was set to NaN and ignored during averaging (Cui and Herrmann, 2023; Herrmann 241 

and Ryan, 2024; Cui et al., 2025; Herrmann et al., 2025). Gaze-dispersion time courses for the 242 

left and right eye were averaged. 243 

Continuous pupil-size and gaze-dispersion data were divided into single-trial time 244 

courses ranging from -1 to 6 s for Experiment 1 and -1 to 8.2 s for Experiments 2-4 time-locked to 245 

babble onset. Data for an entire trial were excluded from analysis if the percentage of NaN data 246 

entries made up more than 50% of the trial. Pupil size and gaze dispersion were averaged across 247 

trials, separately for each SNR condition. 248 

Pupil size and gaze dispersion were analyzed with and without baseline correction. Non-249 

baseline-corrected data enable examination of preparatory, sustained effects, whereas 250 

baseline-corrected data enable examination of trial-evoked responses. For the baseline-251 

correction analysis, data were normalized relative to the -1 to 0 s prior to the onset of the babble 252 

noise by subtracting the mean baseline data from the data at each time point. 253 

For statistical analyses, gaze dispersion was averaged across the 2.8–5.3-s (Experiment 254 

1) or 1.5–4-s (Experiments 2-4) time window during which sentences were presented. Mean pupil 255 

size was calculated for a time window that was delayed by 0.5 s to 3.3–5.8-s for Experiment 1 256 

and 2–4.5-s for Experiments 2-4, because the pupil size is known to change relatively slowly and 257 

peak late during sentence listening (Knapen et al., 2016; Winn and Moore, 2018; Winn et al., 258 

2018; Kadem et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2022). Data were also analyzed for two other time 259 

windows in order to investigate differences in eye metrics during periods when no active 260 

listening engagement was required to solve the main speech-comprehension task. These 261 

included the –1 to 0 s baseline time window (all experiments; non-baseline-corrected data) and 262 

a post-sentence time window (6–8 s) for Experiments 2-4. 263 

Statistical analyses were carried out in JASP software (v0.19.1.0; JASP, 2024). Separate 264 

rmANOVAs were calculated for different measures (pupil size, gaze dispersion) and time 265 

windows, using the within-participants factor SNR (easy, difficult, impossible). Post hoc tests for 266 

significant main effects were calculated using Holm’s method to correct for multiple 267 

comparisons (Holm, 1979). 268

Experiment 1 269 

Experiment 1 aimed to replicate the finding that listeners show reduced eye movements during 270 

impossible-to-understand speech compared to easy-to-understand speech (Herrmann and 271 

Ryan, 2024); but this time in the absence of any visual stimulation. This experiment aimed to 272 

establish whether the previously observed reduction in eye movements was influenced by the 273 

concurrent visual input used in our earlier work (Herrmann and Ryan, 2024), or whether the 274 

effect also occurs when no visual stimuli are present. 275 

Methods and materials 276 

Participants 277 

Sample sizes were based on our previous work (Cui and Herrmann, 2023; Herrmann and Ryan, 278 

2024). Twenty-four adults participated in Experiment 1 (median age: 25 years, age range: 18–35 279 

years; 11 male, 12 female, 1 transgender male). Data from one additional participant were 280 

recorded but not analyzed because their proportion of correct responses for the easiest 281 

condition in the behavioral task was equal to or lower than 0.6, indicating low task compliance. 282 

Stimulation and procedures 283 

Stimulation and procedures were as described above. Participants listened to speech in babble 284 

at easy, difficult, and impossible SNRs while the computer screen was blank. Information 285 

provided to participants was “Comprehension of the next 12 sentences will likely be: ” followed 286 

by “easy”, “difficult”, or “impossible”. They performed a semantic-relatedness task to test for 287 

sentence comprehension. Pupil size and gaze dispersion were calculated for epochs ranging 288 

from -1 s to 6 s, time-locked to noise onset. 289

Summary 320 

Experiment 1 showed the expected listening-effort profile for pupil size, with larger pupil 321 

responses for difficult speech than for easy and impossible speech (Zekveld and Kramer, 2014; 322 

Ohlenforst et al., 2018; Herrmann and Ryan, 2024). Gaze dispersion was lower for difficult 323 

compared with easy speech, consistent with reduced eye movements during effortful listening 324 

(Contadini-Wright et al., 2023; Cui et al., 2025). Critically, gaze dispersion was also reduced for 325 

impossible compared with easy speech even at the very end of a trial, replicating and extending 326 

prior work in which participants viewed a moving-dot display during listening (Herrmann and 327 

Ryan, 2024). The present experiment shows that this reduction occurs even when the screen is 328 

blank. Thus, reduced eye movements during listening to speech that is impossible to 329 

understand cannot be attributed to suppression of visual exploration caused by concurrent 330 

visual stimulation. Instead, the effect appears to arise independently of visual input, suggesting 331 

that reduced gaze dispersion in the impossible condition may reflect some cognitive process 332 

rather than a response to the visual environment. 333 

Experiment 2 334 

In Experiment 2, we tested whether reduced eye movements in the impossible condition reflect 335 

continued attempts to identify a sentence in noise. Even when speech comprehension is 336 

impossible, participants may still listen for a sentence and attempt to extract it from the babble, 337 

and this continued search could reduce eye movements. Because the sentence is not 338

Comparison of difficulty effect between pupil size and gaze dispersion 573 

In addition to examining whether pupil size and gaze dispersion each capture the full effort 574 

profile, we also asked whether the magnitude of the difficulty effect, defined as the difference 575 

between the easy and difficult conditions, differed between the two metrics. This comparison 576

Discussion 598 

The current study examined how pupil size and eye movements track understanding speech that 599 

is easy, difficult, and impossible to follow. Across four experiments, pupil size and gaze 600 

dispersion showed related but dissociable profiles. Pupil size generally followed the expected 601 

effort pattern, with larger responses during difficult than easy or impossible listening. Gaze 602 

dispersion also decreased during difficult speech processing, consistent with effortful listening, 603 

but showed sustained reductions when comprehension was impossible. Together, the findings 604 

fit with an arousal-related interpretation of pupil size, while suggesting that gaze dispersion 605 

reflects both attentional engagement during active listening and disengagement-related states 606 

when meaningful listening is no longer possible. 607 

Pupil size reflects listening effort 608 

Across experiments, pupil size was generally larger when speech was difficult but still 609 

understandable, compared to when it was easy or impossible, showing the expected inverted u-610 

shape pattern, consistent with previous work (Yerkes and Dodson, 1908; Zekveld and Kramer, 611

effort. However, the pupil-size results also highlight important nuance, since the expected effort 614 

profile was not equally strong across all analyses. In some cases (Figures 3 and 5), especially for 615 

baseline-corrected data, the difficult-versus-easy and difficult-versus-impossible differences 616 

were weaker or did not survive multiple comparison correction. This suggests that pupil 617 

responses in the present paradigm reflected a combination of sustained processes and phasic, 618 

sentence-evoked effort. The SNR conditions were cued and blocked, and participants could 619 

thus anticipate whether a trial would be easy, difficult, or impossible. This prior knowledge may 620 

have allowed them to prepare for difficult listening by increasing arousal – and in turn increasing 621 

the pupil size (Mathôt, 2018; Joshi and Gold, 2020; Strauch et al., 2022) – before and throughout 622 

a trial, and this appeared to contribute to the inverted u-shaped pattern in the current study. 623 

Another notable pupil-size finding was the larger response in the impossible condition 624 

after sentence offset, particularly in baseline-corrected analyses in Experiments 2-4. This 625 

response is unlikely to reflect listening effort due to its late timing during a trial when no speech 626 

could occur. One possibility is that it reflects arousal related to the upcoming behavioral 627 

response. In the impossible condition, participants had to guess whether the probe word was 628 

semantically related to a sentence they could not understand or that was absent. Decision 629 

uncertainty could have increased arousal at trial end, prior to the probe word. Previous studies 630 

with SNRs for which participants seemed disengaged from listening have not provided pupil size 631 

time-courses (Zekveld and Kramer, 2014; Ohlenforst et al., 2017a; Ohlenforst et al., 2018), used 632 

shorter trial structures without the possibility to observe such late pupil responses (Herrmann 633 

and Ryan, 2024), or demonstrated a similar increase for impossible conditions, but did not 634 

statistically analyze it (Wendt et al., 2018). This pattern was robust across experiments and may 635 

thus need be considered when interpreting pupil responses in paradigms that include 636 

disengagement-inducing conditions. 637 

Eye movements reflect attentional engagement during active listening 638 

In all four experiments, we observed a phasic reduction in gaze dispersion for the difficult 639 

compared to the easy condition during the sentence-listening period of a trial. This is consistent 640 

with recent works showing reduced eye movements when speech is masked by background 641 

noise (Contadini-Wright et al., 2023; Cui and Herrmann, 2023; Herrmann and Ryan, 2024; 642 

Mariotti et al., 2026), and during high compared to low memory load (Lipton et al., 1980; Hutton 643 

and Tegally, 2005; Kosch et al., 2018; Walter and Bex, 2021). The current work shows that this 644 

reduction in eye movements occurs even while a video is presented (Experiment 4). 645 

Critically, gaze dispersion did not simply mirror pupil size. The reduction in gaze 646 

dispersion for the difficult compared to the easy condition was specifically tied to the period of 647 

active sentence processing. In none of the experiments did gaze dispersion differ between easy 648

2026; Liu and Chait, 2026). 654 

Pupil size is closely linked to arousal systems, particularly the locus coeruleus– 655 

norepinephrine system (Bradley et al., 2008; Mathôt, 2018; Wang et al., 2018; Joshi and Gold, 656 

2020; Ross and Van Bockstaele, 2021; Burlingham et al., 2022; Strauch et al., 2022; Torres et al., 657

governed by partly overlapping but distinct oculomotor and attentional networks, including 659 

cortical and subcortical structures involved in orienting, visual exploration, and cognitive control 660 

(Sparks, 2002; Pierrot-Deseilligny et al., 2004; Lencer and Trillenberg, 2008; Pierce et al., 2019). 661 

Reduced gaze dispersion during difficult listening may reflect a shift in attentional engagement, 662 

such that listeners may reduce visual exploration to prioritize auditory processing or to minimize 663 

irrelevant visual input while trying to understand masked speech. 664 

Sustained eye-movement reductions may reflect disengagement or inward attention 665 

The most important departure from a simple effort account was the sustained reduction in gaze 666 

dispersion during impossible listening. We had expected a u-shaped profile (Yerkes and Dodson, 667 

1908), such that effort or attentional investment would be similarly low – and gaze dispersion 668 

similarly high – during easy and impossible speech comprehension. This prediction rested on 669 

the assumption that, when no sentence was present, listeners would have little motivation to 670 

attempt comprehension because additional effort could not improve listening success (Brehm 671 

and Self, 1989; Botvinick and Braver, 2015; Herrmann and Johnsrude, 2020). This pattern was 672 

observed in baseline-corrected analyses, particularly in Experiments 3 and 4 (Figures 6B and 673 

8B). However, non-baseline-corrected data in Experiments 2 and 3 showed that gaze dispersion 674 

remained lower throughout the trial in the impossible condition (Figures 4A and 6A). This 675 

indicates that reduced eye movements do not always indicate active attentional engagement or 676 

listening effort. 677 

Experiments 1-3 showed that the reduction in eye movements during impossible listening 678 

occurs even without visual stimulation. Thus, it cannot be explained by suppression of 679 

exploration of a moving-dot display used in previous work (Herrmann and Ryan, 2024). 680 

Experiment 2 and 3 showed that extending the post-sentence interval and removing sentence 681 

presentation did not restore eye movements in the impossible condition. This argues against the 682 

idea that listeners simply continued searching for an inaudible sentence and would resume eye 683 

movements once they inferred that the sentence had ended. Gaze dispersion remained lower 684

We suggest that sustained eye-movement reductions during impossible listening may 687

2018). When participants know that no useful speech information can be extracted, and when 690 

there is little of interest on the screen, they may withdraw from both auditory and visual 691 

engagement. Such a disengaged state could be associated with reduced visual exploration. This 692 

is consistent with studies reporting reduced eye movements during mind wandering in reading 693 

tasks (Reichle et al., 2010; Uzzaman and Joordens, 2011; Mézière et al., 2026; but see Faber et 694

2017; Walcher et al., 2017). Thus, reduced eye movements may arise from two different states: 696 

focused engagement during difficult listening and disengaged inward focus during impossible 697 

listening. The distinction between these different states may depend on timing, baseline 698 

correction, and the broader task context. It is notable, however, that gaze dispersion was lower 699 

and temporally more selective during listening effort in the difficult condition than during 700 

possible disengagement in the impossible condition (Figures 2A, 6A, 8A). 701 

Experiment 4 tested the interpretation that the reduction in eye movements may reflect a 702 

disengaged cognitive state by presenting videos during the trial to encourage visual engagement. 703 

The video manipulation appeared to reduce some of the sustained pre-trial differences in gaze 704 

dispersion, suggesting that visual engagement can partly counteract disengagement-related 705 

reductions in eye movements. However, reduced gaze dispersion in the impossible condition 706 

was not fully eliminated (Figure 8A). One possibility is that the videos were not sufficiently 707 

engaging to maintain external attention throughout blocked impossible trials. The selected clips 708 

were neutral in valence and arousal, and many contained relatively localized visual action 709 

(Davidson et al., 2025). Another possibility is that knowing that no sentence was present 710 

promoted disengagement from the trial as a whole, even in the presence of visual input. Future 711 

work could test this more directly using more engaging visual scenes, trial-by-trial measures of 712 

mind wandering, or conditions in which participants have an explicit visual task during 713 

impossible listening. 714 

Implications for using eye movements as a measure of listening effort 715 

The present findings have two implications for using eye movements as an index of listening 716 

effort. First, gaze dispersion is a sensitive marker of active listening engagement (Contadini-717 

Wright et al., 2023; Cui et al., 2025). Across experiments, gaze dispersion reliably and temporally 718 

selectively decreased during difficult speech processing. In fact, for longer trials and in the 719 

absence of visual stimulation, the difficult-to-easy effect for gaze dispersion was greater than 720 

the effect for pupil size (Experiments 2 and 3; Figure 9). This mirrors the reduced sensitivity of the 721

Second, reduced eye movements (lower gaze dispersion) should not automatically be 726

listening shows that similar eye-movement patterns can arise from different cognitive states. 729

This means that gaze dispersion is most informative when interpreted together with task 733

measures such as pupil size. The dissociation between pupil size and gaze dispersion suggests 735 

the two measures could provide complementary information (cf. Contadini-Wright et al., 2023; 736 

Herrmann and Ryan, 2024; Huviyetli and Chait, 2026; Liu and Chait, 2026): pupil size may be 737 

more closely aligned with arousal-related effort, whereas gaze dispersion may reflect the 738 

allocation of attention between external sensory processing, visual exploration, and internally 739 

oriented states. 740 

Conclusion 741 

Across four experiments, pupil size and gaze dispersion provided complementary indices of 742 

listening-related cognition. Pupil size generally followed the expected inverted u-shape effort 743 

profile, increasing most when speech was difficult but still understandable. Eye movements – as 744 

assessed using gaze dispersion – showed a robust phasic reduction during difficult listening, 745 

consistent with increased attentional engagement during active speech processing. However, 746 

gaze dispersion also remained low during impossible listening in non-baseline-corrected data, 747 

suggesting that reduced eye movements can reflect disengagement or inward attention rather 748 

than effort alone. These findings suggest that eye movements are a sensitive marker of 749 

attentional engagement during listening, but their interpretation depends on context. Combining 750 

pupil size and eye movements may therefore provide a richer account of how listeners allocate 751 

effort, attention, and arousal across changing listening demands. 752
