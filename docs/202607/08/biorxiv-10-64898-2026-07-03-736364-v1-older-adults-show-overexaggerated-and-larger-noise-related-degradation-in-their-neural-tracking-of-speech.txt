Abstract 34 

Background : Speech-in-noise (SIN) perception is a difficult everyday listening task that 35 

becomes more difficult with age. Neural tracking of target speech is associated with 36 

successful speech perception in clean and noise-degraded listening environments. How 37 

aging impacts neural tracking of speech and relates to behavioral decrements in older 38 

adults’ SIN perception remains unclear. To address these questions, we measured 39 

neural speech tracking during a continuous SIN perception task in younger and older 40 

adults via multichannel EEG. 41 

Method : Participants ( n=83) monitored a continuous stream of syllables (~4.5 Hz) 42 

presented in quiet and noise conditions during EEG recordings. We assessed neural 43 

phase-locking value (PLV) to the acoustic speech envelope to investigate interactions 44 

between aging, hearing loss, and stimulus noise on neural synchronization to speech. 45 

Results : Compared to younger adults, older adults demonstrated less behavioral 46 

sensitivity to noise effects than young adults and had higher overall PLV to target 47 

speech. Older adults also showed greater noise-related degradations in neural speech 48 

processing relative to younger listeners. Age remained a strong predictor of behavioral 49 

responses to speech even after controlling for hearing loss. Covarying for hearing loss 50 

removed most age-related effects on neural PLV. 51 

Conclusion : Older adults demonstrate overexaggerated neural tracking to ongoing 52 

speech presented in quiet and greater noise-related reductions in neurobehavioral 53 

speech processing than young adults. Our results support the decline-compensation 54 

hypothesis, corroborate unusually large speech envelope encoding in older listeners, 55 

and suggest more robust neural synchronization to the speech signal is not always 56 

perceptually advantageous. 57

1. Introduction 60 

Everyday listening necessitates the ability to perceive speech-in-noise (SIN), an 61 

already-difficult task that becomes even more difficult with age (Heidari et al., 2018). 62 

SIN difficulties are a common complaint for older adults (> 65 years of age) even 63 

without hearing loss (Cruickshanks et al., 1998; Nash et al., 2011; Pichora-Fuller et al., 64 

2017). Emerging links between declining SIN abilities and risks for social isolation, 65 

depression, and dementia highlight the need for interventions which address age-66 

related SIN deficits. However, current behavioral interventions are lacking and hearing 67 

aids do not adequately address SIN problems in elderly individuals (Beck et al., 2018). 68 

As such, it is imperative to understand the neural and behavioral mechanisms of SIN-69 

related declines in aging to inform the development of targeted interventions. 70 

Difficulties perceiving SIN are perhaps the most common audiologic complaints 71 

in the older adult population (Kochkin, 2010). SIN abilities decline exponentially after 72 

age 50 due to a variety of age-related changes which impact the auditory system from 73 

the cochlea to the cortex, resulting in changes to sensation and perception (Moore et 74 

al., 2014). For example, the encoding of temporal fine structure is important for both SIN 75 

perception (Hopkins & Moore, 2009) and cortical tracking of speech (Ding et al., 2014), 76 

yet decreases with age independent of hearing loss (Hopkins & Moore, 2011). Age-77 

related changes within the central auditory system, including the balance of 78 

excitatory/inhibitory activity (Caspary et al., 2008; Caspary et al., 1995) and large-scale 79 

neural network connectivity (Martin & Jerger, 2005), could account for SIN deficits in 80 

older listeners (Bidelman, Price, et al., 2019; Bidelman et al., 2014). 81 

Though aging and age-related hearing loss (ARHL, presbycusis) both impact SIN 82 

processing (Dubno et al., 1984; Moore et al., 2014; Smith et al., 2024), they are difficult 83 

to separate. These factors show parallel trajectories of decline and impact SIN 84 

processing in similar ways by decreasing access to temporal fine structure cues 85 

(Füllgrabe et al., 2015; Hopkins & Moore, 2011) and reducing the perceptual release 86 

from fluctuating maskers (Gifford et al., 2007; Gregan et al., 2013). However, age and 87 

hearing sensitivity might also be partially separable factors in terms of auditory aging. 88 

For example, hearing loss can actually bolster the detection of envelope modulations 89 

(Bacon & Gleitman, 1992; Moore & Glasberg, 2001; Wallaert et al., 2017). Yet, 90 

enhanced processing of basic amplitude envelope structure does not away transfer to 91 

speech, suggesting it may actually distort communication signals and inhibit recognition. 92 

Sensorineural hearing loss, independent of age, is associated with decreased 93 

peripheral auditory sensitivity alongside central changes in the brain’s white matter 94 

(Tarabichi et al., 2018). Such peripheral and central changes in the auditory system 95 

could lead to poorer fidelity and internal representations of speech (Bidelman, Price, et 96 

al., 2019). From the extant literature, it is clear that both age itself and ARHL reflect 97

overlapping contributions to auditory aging that must be accounted for to fully describe 98

Neural synchronization, or the phase-locking of neural activity to external stimuli 100 

such as speech, is one neural mechanism which underlies successful speech 101 

perception (Ding & Simon, 2014; Vanthornhout et al., 2018). Neural synchronization to 102 

speech seems to be strongest around 4-5 Hz, the purported “nominal speech rate” 103 

across the world’s languages (Assaneo & Poeppel, 2018; Ding et al., 2017; He et al., 104 

2023; Momtaz & Bidelman, 2024). Stronger neural speech tracking relates to improved 105 

speech comprehension both in quiet (Riecke et al., 2018; Vanthornhout et al., 2018) and 106 

noise (Ding et al., 2014; Etard & Reichenbach, 2019; Keshavarzi et al., 2020). Similarly, 107 

deficits in the neural timing of the speech-evoked potentials and unusually large neural 108 

tracking of the speech envelope correlate with poorer behavioral speech recognition 109 

(Momtaz et al., 2021; Roque et al., 2019; Sidiras et al., 2020), highlighting the 110 

importance of temporal encoding for SIN perception. 111 

The ways in which aging and ARHL impact neural envelope tracking and 112 

behavioral perception of SIN are not fully understood (Sauvé et al., 2019; Sauvé et al., 113 

2022; Peelle et al., 2010; Criscuolo et al., 2025). Some studies have found that 114 

enhanced neural envelope encoding of target speech is beneficial for speech 115 

understanding (McClaskey et al., 2019; Petersen et al., 2017; Schmitt et al., 2022). Still, 116 

other studies find that enhanced envelope tracking is related to poorer SIN 117 

comprehension, perhaps reflecting abnormal “central gain,” decreased inhibition, or 118 

increase redundancy of the brain networks subserving speech processing (Bidelman et 119 

al., 2014; Goossens et al., 2018; Millman et al., 2017). Differences in target speech 120 

material, background noise type, and audibility across studies likely lead to diverging 121 

results. However, given the potential for intervention paradigms such as rhythmic cueing 122 

or neurostimulation to improve envelope encoding in tandem with speech perception in 123 

younger listeners (Erkens et al., 2020; Falk et al., 2017; Guilleminot & Reichenbach, 124 

2022; van Bree et al., 2021; Wilsch et al., 2018) and hearing-impaired adults (Erkens et 125 

al., 2021), it is imperative to understand how various factors of the aging process alter 126 

the neural encoding of complex speech signals. 127 

In the present study, we investigated age- and noise-related differences in 128 

cortical speech tracking in an oddball detection paradigm via high-density EEG. Our 129 

dataset comprised a large sample of healthy adults spanning 18-75 years of age and 130 

with and without hearing loss (Bidelman, Mahmud, et al., 2019; Bidelman, Price, et al., 131 

2019; Bidelman et al., 2026; Price & Bidelman, 2021). Speech tokens were presented in 132 

a repetitive speech stream at the nominal speech rate of 4.5 Hz (Assaneo & Poeppel, 133 

2018) across clean and noise conditions. We measured perceptual sensitivity and 134 

neural phase-locking to the running speech to quantify neural-speech synchronization 135 

and noise-related changes in speech processing among younger and older adults. 136

2. Methods 137 

2.1 Participants 138 

The dataset consisted of N=83 participants aggregated from our previously published 139 

EEG studies on speech processing and auditory system function across the lifespan 140 

(Bidelman, Mahmud, et al., 2019; Bidelman, Price, et al., 2019; Bidelman et al., 2026; 141 

Price & Bidelman, 2021). Participants were split into Younger Adult (YA; n = 33, age 142 

range: 18-41 years, mean ± SD: 68.6 ± 5.7 years old; 16/17 male/female) and Older 143 

Adult (OA; n = 50, age range: 52-75, mean ± SD: 23.6 ± 4.2 years old; 15/35 144 

male/female) groups by splitting the participant sample in half at age 50. All participants 145 

self-reported no history of cognitive or psychiatric disorders and had no more than 146 

moderate hearing loss (pure tone average (PTA) ≤ 45 dB HL) confirmed via audiometric 147 

testing (octave frequencies, 250 – 8000 Hz; Figure 1 ). Hearing was symmetric between 148 

ears within both groups ( t test between ears: OA: p = 0.1, YA: p = 0.09). As expected, 149 

older adults had higher PTA thresholds (mean of 500, 1000, and 2000 Hz) than younger 150 

adults (PTA OA : 21.6 ± 7.9 dB HL; PTA YA : 4.2 ± 4.8 dB HL; t(47.7) = 11.3, p < 0.0001). 151 

None of the participants utilized hearing aids. Cognitive function was not screened. 152 

However, we note that mild cognitive impairment actually increases auditory brainstem 153 

and cortical evoked potentials to speech (Bidelman et al., 2017) which is not observed 154 

in this sample of older listeners (Bidelman, Price, et al., 2019). Other recent studies 155 

have also shown that speech envelope tracking does not vary with cognitive status (Bolt 156 

& Giroud, 2024). 157

2.2 Stimuli and task 170 

Specific details of the speech stimuli and task paradigm are described fully elsewhere 171 

(Bidelman, Mahmud, et al., 2019; Bidelman, Price, et al., 2019; Bidelman et al., 2026; 172 

Price & Bidelman, 2021). Briefly, the primary task consisted of a target speech detection 173 

task during simultaneous EEG acquisition ( Figure 2 ). Participants detected oddball 174 

tokens among a triplet of vowels (/a/, /i/, oddball: /u/ (Price & Bidelman, 2021; Bidelman 175 

et al., 2026) or constant-vowels stimuli (/ba/, /pa/, oddball: /ta/ (Bidelman et al., 2019a, 176 

b) presented in a continuous stream. Individual tokens were presented in a pseudo-177 

random order so that at least two frequent tokens occurred between successive oddball 178 

tokens. There were a total of 2000-3000 standard and 70-210 oddballs, respectively. 179 

Each speech token was 100 ms with a fundamental frequency (F0) of 150 Hz. Tokens 180 

were presented with a jittered interstimulus interval with an average rate of ~4.5 Hz (i.e., 181 

1/0.225 ms = 4.44 Hz). Speech stimuli were presented in both clean and noise (+5 or 10 182 

dB SNR; 8-talker babble) blocks. In the original studies, SNRs were determined via pilot 183 

testing and set to avoid ceiling and floor effects while achieving ~60-80% behavioral 184 

performance in both younger and older adults. Target speech was always presented at 185 

75 dB A SPL. Stimulus presentation was controlled through MATLAB (The MathWorks, 186 

Inc.; Natick, MA) via a TDT RP2 interface (Tucker-Davis Technologies; Alachua, FL). 187 

Stimuli were delivered binaurally via insert earphones (Etymotic Research; Elk Grove 188 

Village, IL). 189 

Participants were asked to respond via button press each time they detected the 190 

oddball target. Reaction time (RT) and signal detection accuracy (hits/misses) were 191 

logged for each trial. “Hits” were defined as the presence of a response within 5 token 192 

presentations of the oddball target (Price & Bidelman, 2021). 193

2.3 EEG recording and preprocessing 197 

EEG pre-processing was conducted in MATLAB (The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA). 198 

EEGs were originally recorded from electrodes placed at 10-10 locations on the scalp 199 

(Oostenveld & Praamstra, 2001) and digitized > 5 kHz. However, the data were re-200 

montaged to a common 37 channel electrode configuration (Fpz, Fp1/2, Fz, F3/4, F7/8, 201 

F9/10, FC5/6, FC1/2, T7/8, T9/10, Cz, C3/4, CP1/2, CP5/6, Pz, P3/4, P7/8, P9/10, Oz, 202 

O1/2, A1/A2) via interpolation (Desjardins, 2010) and downsampled to 250 Hz to equate 203 

channel counts and sampling rates across datasets. EEGs were re-referenced to linked 204 

mastoids (M1/M2) offline for subsequent analysis. 205 

Continuous EEGs were then bandpass filtered from 2-50 Hz to isolate phase-206 

locking to the speech envelope and epoched into 10-sec segments relative to target 207 

onsets (e.g., He et al., 2023). 500 epochs from each recording were then averaged to 208 

obtain evoked waveforms for each channel and equate the overall duration of data for 209 

PLV analyses across participants. 210 

2.4 Electrophysiological data analysis 211 

To assess brain-to-speech synchronization, we analyzed neural phase-locking value 212 

(PLV) to the speech stimulus (He et al., 2023; Lachaux et al., 1999). Procedures were 213 

similar to those described in He et al. (2023). PLV describes the correspondence 214 

between two signals by measuring the average phase consistency over time. To 215 

compute frequency-specific PLV, we first bandpass filtered the stimulus and EEG 216 

signals at each electrode around each frequency bin (±1 Hz) from 1 to 30 Hz (0.5 Hz 217 

steps). Signals were then windowed with 500 ms ramps to reduce onset/offset 218 

transients and the instantaneous phase was extracted using the Hilbert transform. PLV 219 

was then calculated in each narrow frequency band according to Equation 1: 220

2.5 Behavioral data 233 

To analyze behavioral responses in the speech detection task, we calculated signal 234 

detection metrics including d-prime ( d′), hits ( H), false alarm ( FA ) rate, and response 235 

bias ( c) (Green & Swets, 1966; Macmillan & Creelman, 2005) to isolate listeners’ 236 

perceptual sensitivity from their decision criteria. A higher d′ [d′ = z( H) – z( FA )] 237 

represents greater sensitivity to detect oddballs within the continuous speech stream. 238 

Higher FA indicates more frequent errors when oddball tokens were not present but 239 

incorrectly reported as such. Higher bias ( c) values ( c = -0.5*[z( H) + z( FA )]) indicate a 240 

more conservative response (e.g., reduced tendency to respond “signal present” even 241 

when the oddball token was present). Cases where listeners obtain perfect accuracy 242 

(i.e., H = 1, FA = 0) imply a d′ of infinity. In these instances, hits and false alarms were 243 

corrected to 0.99 and 0.01, respectively, in order to compute finite signal detection 244 

metrics (Macmillan & Creelman, 2005). 245 

2.6 Speech-in-noise perception 246 

To assess relationships between our primary task and clinically-normed speech-in-noise 247 

perception, we administered two lists of the Quick Speech-in-Noise (QuickSIN) test 248 

(Killion et al., 2004). Participants repeat low-context sentences presented in increasing 249 

levels of background noise. This test results in a score reflecting SNR loss, reflecting 250 

the dB SNR for 50% keyword recall performance (0-3 dB SNR loss is considered 251 

normal). 252 

2.7 Statistical analysis 253 

Mixed-model ANOVAs were performed on the dependent variables (PLV, d′, FA, and c) 254 

in R (version 4.2.2, R-Core-Team 2020) using the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015). 255 

Models utilized fixed predictors of group (2 levels; OA vs. YA) and SNR (2 levels; clean 256 

vs. noise), with subject-level random intercepts. Effect sizes are reported as partial eta 257 

squared ( 𝜂𝜂 𝑝𝑝 2 ) and degrees of freedom ( d.f. ) using Satterthwaite’s method. Tukey 258 

adjustments were used to correct for multiple pairwise comparisons. 259 

We assessed relationships between behavioral, neural, and demographic 260 

variables using Spearman correlations ( r s). For these analyses, we considered noise-261 

related decrements in each neural and behavioral index ( e.g., ∆PLV = PLV clean –262 

PLV noise ; ∆d′ = d′clean – d′noise ). Change scores indexed the amount of degradation to 263 

brain and behavioral speech processing measures introduced by noise. PTA was 264 

positively correlated with age ( r = 0.76, p < 0.0001; data not shown), indicating older 265 

adults had (expectedly) more hearing loss. Consequently, we partialled out PTA in the 266 

correlations to assess the impact of age on neural and behavioral measures 267 

independent of age-related presbycusis. 268

3.5 Model EEG simulations of PLV 352 

To assess whether PLV effects in our empirical data might be explained by more trivial 353 

differences in stimulus acoustics, we simulated oscillatory EEG responses to our 354 

speech train stimuli using a simple convolution model of evoked potential generation 355 

(e.g., Goldstein & Kiang, 1958; Janssen et al., 1991). The model is based on the 356 

assumption that sustained auditory evoked potentials are a series of overlapping onset 357 

evoked responses generated by repeating events in a running stimulus (e.g., Bidelman, 358 

2015; Bidelman et al., 2024; Daly et al., 1976; Dau, 2003; Davis & Hirsh, 1974; 359 

Galambos et al., 1981; Gerken et al., 1975; Janssen et al., 1991; Picton et al., 1977). 360 

The approach is similar to that described in our previous papers (Bidelman, 2015; 361 

Bidelman et al., 2024) and is akin to the temporal response function (TRF) analysis 362 

(Crosse et al., 2021) recently popularized in the field. 363

the continuous acoustic envelope of the speech signal ( Fig. 7A ). We modelled the 365 

unitary TRF describing the canonical auditory cortical potentials (i.e., “P1-N1-P2” 366 

complex) as a sum of Gaussian functions (Equation 2): 367

4. Discussion 387 

Using EEG, we assessed how aging impacts neural synchronization to and 388 

identification of target speech in clean and noise-degraded listening conditions. We 389 

found that: (a) older adults (OA) demonstrated fewer behavioral differences between 390 

clean and noise than did younger adults (YA); (b) OA demonstrated greater noise-391 

related reductions in neural speech tracking speech than YA, despite having 392 

overexaggerated neural tracking of clean speech; and (c) age accounted for behavioral 393 

(but not neural) deficits in speech processing beyond the effects of hearing loss. Lastly, 394 

computational modeling suggested these age-related differences are not due to 395 

acoustic stimulus properties alone but instead reflect changes in the neurobiological 396 

synchronization to complex sounds with advancing age. 397 

4.1 Aging is associated with less behavioral sensitivity to noise-related changes 398 

In a speech-oddball detection paradigm, YA showed expected noise decrements in 399 

perception whereas OA performed similarly across SNRs (i.e., ∆d′ OA < ∆ d′YA ). The more 400 

subtle behavioral changes seen in OA aligns with the reduced release from masking 401 

and gap listening observed in elderly listeners (Dubno et al., 2002; Feng et al., 2018; 402 

Irsik et al., 2022; Zobel et al., 2019). OA also had higher overall false alarm (FA) rates 403

4.2 Age-related differences in auditory neural synchronization to speech 427 

4.2.1. Older adults show overexaggerated PLV to clean speech 428 

Contrasting the behavioral results, OA demonstrated overexaggerated neural 429 

synchronization (PLV) to syllable trains presented in clean, and consequently, greater 430 

reductions in PLV with noise. Electrophysiological and psychoacoustic findings in both 431 

animals and humans show that aging and hearing loss are associated with increased 432 

envelope coding (Bacon & Gleitman, 1992; Kale & Heinz, 2010; Moore & Glasberg, 433 

2001; Parthasarathy et al., 2014; Schmitt et al., 2022). Aging and ARHL are inextricably 434 

tied and often manifest in overlapping effects (see Sect. 4.3. ). One possibility is that 435 

stronger neural responses in elderly individuals could reflect “central gain” mechanisms 436 

(Chambers et al., 2016) which help offset the reduced sensory input (Millman et al., 437 

2017) associated with auditory aging. Such overexaggerated envelope encoding could 438 

reflect a general imbalance between excitatory/inhibitory processes in the central 439 

auditory nervous system (e.g., Caspary et al., 2008; Caspary et al., 1995). Alternatively, 440 

increased neural tracking of speech in older adults may be related to increased listening 441

4.2.2. Older adults demonstrated greater noise-related degradation in neural 461

Despite stronger neural tracking of clean speech, OA experienced greater reductions in 463

exaggerated speech envelope encoding paired with greater noise-related reductions in 465

attributes which are easily degraded by background noise. 471

not titrated based on individual performance, and the SNR differed slightly across 476 

datasets, it is notable we observe differences in OA neural synchronization even to 477 

clean speech envelopes. Similar results have been observed in recent studies in both 478

4.3 Relationships between age, hearing loss, and speech tracking 487 

As expected, we observed a strong relationship between age and hearing loss in nearly 488 

all of our speech measures. Though age and hearing are sometimes separable in 489 

describing speech processing in older adults (Babkoff & Fostick, 2017; Bidelman et al., 490 

2014; Füllgrabe et al., 2015), our data confirm the multidimensional and highly 491 

interconnected nature of factors that impact auditory aging (Bugannim et al., 2025; 492 

Humes et al., 2013; Humes et al., 2012; Lentz et al., 2022). Here, we show that after 493 

partialling for hearing acuity, the relationship between age and neural ∆PLV disappears. 494 

Similarly, age considered jointly with hearing loss contributed substantial shared 495 

variance that explained EEG and perceptual responses and had more explanatory 496 

power than when considered separately. Thus, age by itself did not uniquely explain 497 

changes in neural tracking. In contrast, we found that the relationship between age and 498 

perceptual ∆d′ remained significant even after controlling for hearing loss. These results 499 

together suggest that age was a unique (albeit small) predictor of noise-related 500 

differences in behavioral speech processing. In their systematic review specifically on 501 

speech tracking, Ratelle and Tremblay (2025) similarly conclude that both age and 502 

hearing acuity interdependently modulate neural synchronization. Though we did not 503 

screen for cognitive functioning, cognition is unlikely to account for our group effects as 504 

speech envelope tracking does not vary with cognitive status (Bolt & Giroud, 2024). 505 

Our data highlight the importance of considering both age and ARHL when 506 

assessing speech synchronization in older listeners. This is particularly important for 507 

studies attempting to enhance speech envelope tracking and improve SIN processing 508 

via neurostimulation techniques (e.g., Erkens et al., 2020; Guilleminot & Reichenbach, 509 

2022; van Bree et al., 2021; Wilsch et al., 2018). The present results suggest speech 510 

envelope tracking in older listeners is already hyperactive in a baseline state. When 511 

hearing acuity is considered, some studies find that age and ARHL uniquely contribute 512 

to differences in neural speech tracking (Bidelman et al., 2014; McClaskey, 2024; 513 

Petersen et al., 2017), while others do not (McClaskey et al., 2019). It is possible that 514 

discrepancies across studies could be in part due to stimulus audibility. When hearing 515 

thresholds are not well-controlled or accounted for, the relative contributions of aging 516 

and presbycusis can be confounded in speech tracking measures. The amount of 517 

shared variance between age and hearing loss seen in our neural PLV data 518

4.4 Ramifications for temporal cuing interventions to improve SIN in older 532 

listeners 533 

OA demonstrated less behavioral distinction between speech presented in clean vs. 534 

noise, greater noise-related degradations in neural synchronization to speech, and 535 

poorer SIN perception as assessed via the QuickSIN. Though enhanced envelope 536 

encoding is often assumed to improve speech perception (Decruy et al., 2019; 537 

Vanthornhout et al., 2018), the overexaggerated responses we find in older listeners 538 

(even for even quiet speech) tempers this assertion. Still, SIN deficits and poorer 539 

auditory temporal processing are among the most common hearing deficits in OA 540 

(Anderson et al., 2012; Füllgrabe et al., 2015; Gordon-Salant et al., 2011). 541 

Consequently, our data prompt questions on whether rehabilitation strategies to bolster 542 

temporal envelope processing might be beneficial in older individuals. Indeed, promising 543 

studies suggest that timing-based rate discrimination training can partially restore age-544 

related deficits in temporal processing (Anderson et al., 2022). Cueing the speech 545 

envelope has also been shown to improve neural tracking to and perception of SIN in 546 

young adults (Falk et al., 2017; te Rietmolen et al., 2025). Relatedly, neurostimulation 547 

(e.g., tDACs, TMS) and multimodal stimuli (e.g., audio-haptic presentation) that aim to 548 

enhance envelope tracking by mirroring sound with other concurrent signals are also 549 

encouraging (e.g., Erkens et al., 2020; Guilleminot & Reichenbach, 2022; van Bree et 550 

al., 2021; Wilsch et al., 2018). However, putative benefits have not yet been widely 551 

explored in older listeners. It is possible that temporal cueing of SIN could minimize the 552 

greater amount of noise-related degradation seen in speech synchronization in older 553 

ears. Alternatively, there may be a fundamental limit on to the degree to which speech 554 

envelope enhancement remains viable for perception in older listeners, as suggested by 555 

our data. Future studies should investigate the potential benefits and limits of temporal 556 

cueing to restore envelope encoding in the aging auditory system. 557
