## 摘要
儿童在概率性统计学习任务中的表现往往优于成人，但这一发展优势背后的机制仍缺乏充分理解。在本研究中，我们使用基于眼动追踪的信念更新测量方法，考察儿童与成人如何在概率性序列学习任务中获得并更新预测。采用标准的（眼动）反应时指标时，儿童表现出比成人更强的统计学习能力，这不仅重复了以往的行为学发现，也揭示了统计学习发展差异的更细致特征。关键在于，儿童更频繁地更新其预测：他们较少重复先前的预测，更倾向于根据新的输入调整预期。相比之下，成人表现出更强的持续性，即使已有预测与潜在统计结构不一致，他们也倾向于维持原有预测。尽管两组在更新行为上存在显著差异，但在预测误差的加工与利用方面却表现出惊人的相似性。这些发现表明，统计学习中的发展差异并非主要源于预测误差如何被计算，而是源于在信念更新过程中如何权衡先验信念与输入信息。儿童增强的学习能力因此可能反映了他们对稳定先验的依赖较低、对当前感觉证据的敏感性更高，从而支持一种更具探索性的学习策略。相反，成人似乎更倾向于采用一种利用性策略，该策略能够稳定已有预测，但会降低其在概率性环境中的灵活性。更广泛而言，研究结果表明，统计学习中的发展变化可能反映了不同年龄学习者在面对新证据时修正预测意愿上的差异。通过结合敏感的眼动测量与探究信念更新机制的分析，本研究更细致地揭示了预测性学习如何随发展而变化，并为协调此前统计学习领域中不一致的发展性研究结果提供了一个框架。

## Abstract
Children often outperform adults in probabilistic statistical learning tasks, yet the mechanisms underlying this developmental advantage remain poorly understood. Here, we used eye-tracking measures of belief updating to examine how children and adults acquire and update predictions in a probabilistic sequence-learning task. Using the standard (oculomotor) reaction time measure, children showed stronger statistical learning than adults, replicating previous behavioral findings while revealing a more detailed profile of developmental differences in statistical learning. Critically, children updated their predictions more frequently: they were less likely to repeat previous predictions and more likely to shift their expectations in response to new input. Adults, in contrast, showed greater persistence, tending to maintain prior predictions even when those predictions were inconsistent with the underlying statistical structure. Despite these pronounced differences in updating behavior, the processing and use of prediction errors were remarkably similar across age groups. These findings indicate that developmental differences in statistical learning do not primarily arise from how prediction errors are computed, but rather from how prior beliefs and incoming information are weighted during belief updating. Childrens enhanced learning may therefore reflect reduced reliance on stable priors and greater sensitivity to current sensory evidence, supporting a more exploratory learning strategy. Adults, by contrast, appear to favor an exploitative strategy that stabilizes existing predictions but reduces flexibility in probabilistic environments. More broadly, the results suggest that developmental changes in statistical learning may reflect age-related differences in how readily learners revise their predictions in response to incoming evidence. By integrating sensitive oculomotor measures with analyses that probe the mechanisms underlying belief updating, the present study provides a more fine-grained account of how predictive learning changes across development and offers a framework for reconciling previously inconsistent developmental findings in statistical learning.
