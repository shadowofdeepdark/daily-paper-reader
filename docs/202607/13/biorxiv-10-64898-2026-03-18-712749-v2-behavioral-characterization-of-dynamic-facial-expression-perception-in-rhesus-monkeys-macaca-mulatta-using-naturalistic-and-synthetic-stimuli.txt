ABSTRACT 14 

Facial displays are an important channel of communication for primates, yet it remains unclear based on 15 

which criteria monkeys evaluate facial expressions. We trained two rhesus macaques to categorize vid eos 16 

of four facial expression types (neutral, lip -smacking, silent bared -teeth (SBT) and open -mouth threat 17 

displays) and then tested generalization to novel individuals and avatars while measuring pupillary 18 

responses. The animals were able to categorize fa cial expressions and generalize their classifications to 19 

novel, untrained videos, although performance was not perfect. Misclassifications were not driven solely 20 

by visual similarity between expression categories, as demonstrated by a novel automated appro ach 21 

integrating deep learning -based motion tracking with the Macaque Facial Action Coding System to enable 22 

objective quantification of facial action units. Instead, open -mouth threats were readily categorized 23 

correctly and distinguished from lip -smacking, while also eliciting the strongest arousal responses. By 24 

contrast, categorization of SBT displays varied substantially across stimulus identities, influenced by body 25 

weight, gaze direction, and coordinated movements of eyebrows and ears. Morphed avatar exp ressions 26 

were categorized according to expression component intensity, demonstrating graded perception. Avatar 27 

manipulations revealed that categorization was robust against lack of coherent motion, lack of realism 28 

beyond a basic level of texture, and to so me extent transcended species form boundaries to a human 29 

face. However, human facial expressions elicited no systematic categorizations or differential arousal. Our 30 

findings indicate that conspecific facial expressions were perceived as functionally meanin gful, context -31 

dependent social signals shaped by all components of facial behavior and signaler characteristics, rather 32 

than fixed , mouth movement -defined categories. 33

INTRODUCTION 36 

The ability to distinguish and interpret facial expressions is an important social skill for all primate species. 37 

Since Darwin (1872) pioneered the systematic investigation of facial displays in humans and animals, many 38 

ethological studies in non -human primates have provided descriptions of facial movements and the 39 

situations in which they usually occur (e.g. Andrew, 1963; Hinde, 1966; Maestripieri, 1997; Partan, 2002; 40 

Preuschoft, 2000; van Hooff, 1967) . Laboratory studies have reported how macaque monkeys visually 41 

scan facials displays (Go thard, Erickson, & Amaral, 2004; Mosher, Zimmerman, & Gothard, 2011; Nahm, 42 

Perret, Amaral, & Albright, 1997; Siebert et al., 2020) and examined neural responses to viewing 43 

conspecific facial expressions (e.g. Gotha rd, Battaglia, Erickson, Spitler, & Amaral, 2007; Hadj -Bouziane, 44 

Bell, Knusten, Ungerleider, & Tootell, 2008; Hasselmo, Rolls, & Baylis, 1989; Hoffman, Gothard, Schmid, & 45 

Logothetis, 2007; J. Taubert et al., 2020; Yang & Freiwald, 2021; Zhu et al., 2013) . The most frequently 46 

employed expressions in these studies were aggressive open -mouth threat, affiliative/appeasing lip -47 

smacking with puckered lips and rapid opening and closing of the mouth, and fearful/submissive fear -grin 48 

or -grimace, more objectively c alled the silent bared -teeth display (SBT), with mouth corners and lips 49 

retracted to expose the teeth. However, despite the widespread use of these stereotypical categories, 50 

few studies have explicitly asked monkeys to behaviorally discriminate facial expr essions. Kanazawa 51 

(1996) reported that one Japanese macaque differentiated facial displays in a match -to -sample paradigm 52 

along a “subordinate – dominant” dimension, distinguishing SBT and threat faces by mouth shape and eye 53 

width, and a “neutral – tense” dimension, separating relaxed faces from faces with raised eyebrows, 54 

whereas the macaque only distinguished the human smile among other human expressions. This study 55 

only used one Japanese macaqu e’s and one human’s displays as stimuli, leaving unclear whether the 56 

subject resorted to any semantic information about expression categories or simply differentiated 57 

dissimilar pictures. Parr and Heintz (2009) performed a similar experiment wi th seven rhesus macaques 58 

and the categories open -mouth threat, SBT, play face and scream face. The macaques struggled when the 59 

identity of the sample, the matching and the non -matching stimulus were all different and confusion 60 

apparently occurred due to sh ared features between expression categories, especially mouth opening 61 

and teeth exposure. However, overlapping features alone could not explain the overall error pattern. 62 

Notably, the macaques exhibited a bias for selecting the play face, presumably the mo st positively valued 63 

among the offered categories. An analogous approach was used by Micheletta, Whitehouse, Parr, and 64 

Waller (2015) who teste d three crested macaques and reported that the visual similarity between 65 

expressions was not correlated with performance, and speculated that functional similarity between 66 

expressions could be responsible for errors. J. Taubert and Japee (2024) trained four rhesus macaques to 67 

select a “positive” facial expression (neutral or lip -smack) over a “negative” expression (threat or SBT). 68 

When tested with novel images, the macaques most frequently selected the lip -smack sti muli and least 69 

frequently selected the threat stimuli, as expected, but also selected two SBT displays over most neutral 70 

faces, while the other three SBTs ranked among the threat displays, suggesting variable valence of SBTs. 71 

All four studies relied on sta tic images, although Micheletta et al. (2015) also included dynamic video 72 

stimuli and anecdotally noted improved performance. 73 

While these studies have demonstrated that macaques are able to distinguish facial expressions in 74 

experimental settings, it remains unclear I) which strategy they used to discriminate expressions and II) 75 

which facial motion, shape and appearance features are necessary and sufficient for the pe rception and 76

differentiation of a facial expression category. To answer these questions, we trained two rhesus 77 

macaques to explicitly discriminate videos of neutral, lip -smacking, SBT and threat displays in a forced -78 

choice categorization paradigm, avoiding a potential bias to select more pleasant expressions in a match -79 

to -sample configuration.  We subsequently assessed them using novel expression videos in catch trials and 80 

measured physiological arousal through pupillary responses.  For part I), the test vide os were filmed real 81 

macaques and a naturalistic monkey avatar. We reasoned that if the macaques used elementary facial 82 

features to make their decisions without attaching any meaning to the videos, they would perform 83 

comparably well across expression catego ries for the new test videos, with mistakes occurring among 84 

visually similar expressions and without physiological reactions to the videos. However, i f the macaques 85 

perceived the videos as the described mouth movement -defined morphological categories (neut ral, lip -86 

smacking, SBT and open -mouth threat), each with discrete meaning, they would exhibit the same 87 

performance and error pattern, but experience differential arousal when seeing the different expression 88 

categories. To quantify similarity among the expr essions, we combined the Facial Action Coding System 89 

(FACS), which allows the objective, muscle -based description of facial movements (Ekman & Friesen, 90 

1978; Ekman, Friesen, & Hager, 2002; Parr, Waller, Burrows, Got hard, & Vick, 2010) and deep learning -91 

based motion tracking (DeepLabCut) (Mathis et al., 2018; Nath et al., 2019) . If the meaning the macaques 92 

extracted from the stimuli is not fully captured by the defined catego ries, inconsistent performance across 93 

expression categories and test identities should occur and visual similarity should fail to explain the 94 

pattern of miscategorizations . I nstead confusions between expressions with similar function should occur, 95 

like lip -smacking and neutral, lip -smacking and SBT or SBT and threat, and a clear distinction between lip -96 

smacking and threat. However, if they only learned the association of the rewarded target for each 97 

individual training video, they should fail to categorize the new videos according to expression type, 98 

although rhesus macaques were able to categorize action videos in a similar paradigm (Cui et al., 2023) .99 

For part II), we made use of a monkey avatar to continuo usly morph one expression into another, to 100 

remove motion coherence, to systematically change the facial appearance by gradually decreasing realism 101 

(no fur, no color, no texture, no shape), to change the species -specific facial form to a human avatar and 102 

to change the species -specific motion pattern to human expressions. Note that we use the conventional 103 

term facial expression interchangeably with facial display, facial configuration, facial behavior or facial 104 

movement pattern, without assuming any expressio n of an emotional state. 105

Experimental paradigm and procedu re 161 

Subjects sat head -restrained in a primate chair 60 cm in front of a computer monitor in a darkened booth. 162 

Stimuli were presented via the NREC open -source control system (https://nrec.neurologie.un i-163 

tuebingen.de/ ) on a gray background. Eye position and pupil size (vertical diameter) were recorded with 164 

an eye tracker (ISCAN ETL -200, 120 Hz). A trial was initiated by fixation on a dot (radius 0.2° visual angle) 165 

for 1 s, followed by a 1–2 s centrally presented video (12° x 12°) and 0.5 s of blank screen. Then four 166 

differently colored targets (0.4° radius) appeared in the screen corners (each 12° horizontally and 7° 167 

vertically from the screen center). Subjects indicated the perceived expression category via a saccade to 168 

the corresponding target (detection window 6° x 6°) (Fig. 1). Target -category associations were learned 169 

through reward contingencies assigned by the experimenter. Eye positions outside the bounds of the 170 

video or failure to saccade to any of the targets after 10 s aborted the trial. During the training phase, 171 

subjects were only rewarded for choosing the correct target. T ask t raining started with expression 172 

categories separately in blocks with a single target and a subset of stimuli , then su ccessively more stimuli 173 

and more targets were added and more categories were nested until full randomization of trials was 174 

reached. The training procedure was flexibly adapted to then animal’s current needs and abilities. Once 175 

performance stably exceeded 7 0 % per category (Supplementary Fig. S1A -B) , the test phase was started 176 

with new videos presented interleaved with the trained videos in 5 -10 % catch trials, in which choice of 177 

any target was rewarded. The performance on the trained videos throughout the t est phase is shown in 178 

Supplementary Fig. S1C -D. In part I of the test phase, all new real macaque videos and the monkey avatar 179 

with prototypical expressions were tested. Then , the subjects were specifically re -trained on the 180 

prototypical expression avatar videos for approximately two weeks, which were subsequently 181 

incorporated in to the training set. In part II, the morphed avatar expressions, the scrambled -frame avatar, 182 

the point -light avatar, the degraded realism avatars and the human avatar were tested. T he morphed test 183 

stimuli were presented in 9.9 % of avatars trials (among the prototypical avatar expressions), nested 184

After the monkeys had been trained to categorize videos of conspecifics showing neutral, lip -smacking, 308

Interactions between face parts and additional social information influence the categorization behavior 366 

Examination of the categorization performances fo r each test identity in Fig. 3A and of the individual 367 

performances of both monkeys (Supplementary Fig. S 2) suggested that the monkeys used idiosyncratic 368 

categorization strategies for different test identities. This was confirmed by an MLR analysis with 369 

“Id entity” added to “Expression” as predictors of the target choice, which significantly improved the 370 

model fit (MLR Model 3: ΔD = 1184.44 , p < 0.001 ; Supplementary Table S2 and Fig. 4A). We therefore 371 

wondered what factors exactly in the faces and facial move ments of the different test identities might 372 

lead to these differences in categorization. We hypothesized that subtle variations in the exact facial 373 

movement compositions (eyebrow and ear movements, degree of mouth movements), information about 374 

the monkey’ s perceived strength, or direct vs. averted gaze might cause facial expressions to be perceived 375 

differently, and that the two monkey subjects might have pursued individual categorization strategies. To 376 

test these hypotheses, we compared different MLR model s in a step -wise procedure, the results of which 377 

are summarized in Supplementary Table S2 and Fig. 4A. In order to describe facial movements agnostic of 378 

a priori category labels, we used the MaqFACS framework to identify and quantify activated facial actio n379 

units (AUs) and thus deconstruct the facial expressions into their muscle movement components. The 380 

composite FACS predictors (MLR Model 4: D = 9048.91, Chi 2(21, 4086) = 1584.04, p < 0.001 ) yielded similar 381 

explanatory power as using the expression categor y predictor (MLR Model 1: D = 9039.40, Chi 2(9, 4086) =382 

1593.55, p < 0.001 ). However, including three -way interactions between the mouth predictors 383

DISCUSSION 627 

We investigated how rhesus macaques categorize facial expressions, and which facial features enable 628 

expression perception. In a forced -choice categorization par adigm with neutral, lip -smacking, silent 629 

bared -teeth (SBT) and open -mouth threat displays, two rhesus macaques successfully applied learned 630 

categories to unfamiliar individuals and avatars. Pupil size measurements showed graded physiological 631 

responses acro ss expression types, demonstrating that the macaques extracted behavioral meaning 632 

rather than simply sorting low -level visual patterns. When viewing expressions morphed between two 633 

prototypes, categorization frequencies tracked the proportional intensity o f each expression component, 634 

indicating perception along a continuum. To quantify similarity between expressions, we developed a 635 

novel automated approach combining DeepLabCut landmark tracking with the MaqFACS framework, 636 

enabling objective, muscle -based qu antification of all facial movements in frontal videos of monkey faces 637 

without being limited to mouth movements only (Blumrosen, Hawellek, & Pesaran, 2017) or requiring 638 

pre -labeled training datasets (Morozov, Parr, Gothard, Paz, & Pryluk, 2021) . This approach showed that 639 

FACS -based visual similarity between expressions alone did not explain error patterns . Instead, 640 

categorization was influenced by how facial regions (m outh, eyebrows, ears) moved in coordination, 641 

whether gaze was averted , and by signaler characteristics exemplified by the displaying individual's body 642 

weight. Th is suggests that the tested expression s types were not interpreted as rigid categories with one 643 

meaning, but instead category -defining movements were interpreted in a nuanced manner embedded 644 

within their entire facial and signaler contexts. Systematic manipulation of avatar properties showed that 645 

neither temporally coherent motion, nor photorealisti c rendering or a conspecific face were essential for 646 

expression recognition, but discernible facial shape and surface texture were required. Notably, human 647 

expressions displayed by human and monkey avatars failed to elicit systematic categorization or 648 

diff erential arousal .649

Categorization of facial expressions does not follow clear morphological categories 651 

In part I, we show that two rhesus macaques trained to categorize facial expressions into neutral, lip -652 

smacking, silent bared -teeth (SBT) and open -mouth threat displays c ould to some extent generalize these 653 

learned categories to new instances of those expressions presented by new agents, both real monkeys 654 

and monkey avatars. Critically, viewing these expressions was accompanied by differential arousal as 655 

measured by pupil size, with incremental pupillary responses to neutral, lip -smacking, SBT and threatening 656 

faces. Addressing our first hypothesis regarding discrimination strategy, the differential physiological 657 

arousal precludes that the macaques resorted to the categorization of elementary facial movement 658 

features without attaching any meaning to the expressions. If they had relied solely on low -level visual 659 

features, we would have expected comparable performance across expression categories without 660 

physio logical differentiation. Instead, the pattern of arousal responses , which was not explained by 661 

motion intensity per se , particularly the robust arousal to threatening faces, suggests that these videos 662 

were perceived as meaningful categories rather than arb itrary visual patterns. However, the 663 

categorization patterns did not align with a perception of the expressions as clear , mouth -movement -664

After integrating mouth, eyeb row and ear movements, gaze direction and signaler characteristics such as 683 

body weight, which criterium could the macaques have used to make their categorization choice? One 684 

possibility is that the y evalu ated the facial displays based on the expressed putative internal states. Ever 685 

since Darwin (1872) systematically des cribed facial expressions and proposed that they might relate to 686 

inner states, facial behavior in humans has been directly linked to the expression of discrete emotions and 687 

widely assumed to be universal (Ekman, 197 3, 1992; Ekman & Friesen, 1971; Izard, 1994; Russell, 1994) .688 

This notion has only recently been questioned, with growing acknowledgement of the amenability of facial 689 

expressions to cultural learning and modulation by individual variation and context (e.g. Barrett, Adolphs, 690 

Marsella, Martinez, & Pollak, 2019; Durán & Fernández -Dols, 2021; Le Mau et al., 2021; Valente, Theurel, 691 

& Gentaz, 2018) . Considering that macaques as nonverbal animals lack semantic emotion conc epts, they 692 

might sort facial expression images similarly to human dementia patients, who have lost semantic 693 

emotion concepts, categorizing them by valence into negative, positive and neutral faces (Lindquist, 694 

Gendron, Barrett, & D ickerson, 2014) . If the macaques classified the videos according to expressed affect, 695 

this would predict a clear separation of positive (presumably lip -smacking) and neutral faces, contrary to 696 

our observations. Another possibility is that they sorted the videos according to perceived behavioral 697 

intent (Crivelli & Fridlund, 2019; Fridlund, 1994; Frijda & Tcherkassof, 1997; Waller, Whitehouse, & 698 

Micheletta, 2017) , similarly to a crested macaque ( M. nigra ) that w as ab le to predict social interaction 699 

outcomes from facial expressions (Waller, Whitehouse, & Micheletta, 2016) . The Relational Model of 700 

conflict resolution distinguishes tolerant, avoidant and aggressive intent i n social decision making (de 701 

Waal & Aureli, 1997) . This would explain the lack of differentiation between neutral faces and lip -702 

smacking, as both might convey tolerance of the other, whereas the open -mouth threat display appears 703 

to unequivocally conve y aggression. The SBT display with exposed teeth and pulled back lip corners has 704 

traditionally been described as negatively valued in the majority of anthropoid species, including the 705

In part II, we exploited a fully controllable monkey avatar to map out which facial motion and shape 745 

features are important for the perception and differentiation of the four facial expression categories. The 746 

categorization frequenci es of expressions morphed between two prototypical expression s closely 747 

followed the choice frequencies to be expected according to the intensity of each expression category in 748 

the respective video. Moreover, increasing threat intensity was paralleled by in creasing pupil size. This 749 

suggests that expressions were perceived along a continuum beyond simple template matching. This 750 

graded sensitivity may reflect the m acaque s’ ability to extract multifaceted information from natural facial 751 

displays, as behavioral contexts require assessment of expression strength, e.g. distinguishing mild threat 752 

from severe aggression, or evaluation of blended signals, e.g. appeasement displays despite aggressive 753 

impulses or threat displays despite fearful disposition. Random fores t classification using the 754 

automatically quantified composite FACS predictors showed that the degree of jaw opening and teeth 755 

baring expectedly predicted the choice probability of the Threat and SBT categories, respectively, and 756 

revealed that lowered brows in ambiguous morphed expressions often informed the choice of the 757 

Lipsmack instead of the Neutral target, and the choice of the SBT instead of the Threat target, whereas 758 

erected ears served as a predictor for the choice of the Threat target. Under the ass umption that the SBT 759 

category in our study was predominantly interpreted as a submissive signal to avoid conflict ( cSBTs )760 

(Beisner & McCowan, 2014; Flack & de Waal, 2007) , lowering the brows and thus protecting the eye region 761 

against an imminent threat would be an adaptive behavior (Andrew, 1963) . Conversely, raising of the 762 

brows during SBTs given as signs of formal subordination (pSBTs ) could serve to enlarge the visual field 763 

for attentive monitoring of more distant possible threats, as has been implicated for human wide -eyed 764 

fear expressions (Susskind et al., 2008) . However, given the restricted nature of our stimulus set and the 765 

small sample size of two monkeys, these conclusions should be taken with caution and used as the basis 766 

for further research explicitly probing and manipulating the role of eyebrow and ear movements in facial 767 

displays, which are currently underexplored in the literature. Anecdotal accounts of eye brow movements 768 

in macaques describe lowered eyebrows combined with a staring gaze, as well as “eyebrow -flashes” (brief 769 

raising of the eyebrows) as low -grade threats, unless combined with lip -smacking (Fortman, Hewett, & 770 

Halliday, 2001; Wolfensohn & Lloyd, 2003) . Rincon et al. (2023) examined the specificity of FACS AU use 771 

in rhesus macaques across different contexts and found that t hey raised their brows mostly in aggressive 772 

contexts and less so in submissive or affiliative situations, whereas lowered eyebrows were almost 773 

exclusively displayed in submissive contexts. Erected ears (EAU1 or EAU2) occurred in aggressive 774 

encounters, wher eas flattened ears were registered in all three contexts. In a similar vein, Partan (2002) 775 

reported that pant -threat vocalizations were most frequently accompanied by forward facing ears. 776 

Humans possess little ear muscle control and thus ear movements do not play a role in facial expressions, 777 

whereas human eyebrow movements are extensive and nuanced, but do not seem diagnostic for a specific 778 

facial expression category (Mielke et al., 2022) . It has been suggested that eyebrows are moved according 779 

to the momentary appropriate biological function of increasing or decreasing the visual input (Ekman, 780 

1979, 2004; Susskind et al., 2008) .781

Methods: Generation of avatars 1089 

The creation of the dynamic monkey head avatar is described in detail elsewhere (Siebert et al., 2020; N. 1090 

Taubert et al., 2021) . In short, the monkey head model was based on MRI scans transformed into a mesh 1091 

surface, which was controlled by embedded ribbons modeled after the macaque's facial muscles (Parr et 1092 

al., 2010) . The ribbon control joints corresponded to motion -captured infrared markers, which had been 1093 

attached to a monkey’s face in order to record the facial movem ents with a VICON FX20 Motion Capture 1094 

System. Human facial movements were recorded analogously from a 40 -year -old male human subject. 1095 

The segmented movement trajectories were preprocessed with VICON Nexus 1.85 software and time -1096 

normalized and smoothed with a generative Bayesian method combining Gaussian process latent variable 1097 

models and Gaussian process dynamic models, which allows for morphing between expressions (see N. 1098 

Taubert et al. (2021) Appendix 1 for detail s on the algorithm). The textures and skin material of the 1099 

monkey avatar were hand -painted using photographic references, and additional texture maps were 1100 

created in Adobe Photoshop to represent various skin layers. The fur was simulated with Autodesk Maya ’s 1101 

XGen Interactive Grooming tool. All movie frames were generated with Autodesk Arnold Renderer. The 1102 

human avatar was based on a female face scan and texture maps provided by EISKO, with custom 1103 

adjustments to the color and texture maps to match the monkey head model. The movement 1104 

deformations were achieved via control points equivalent to the monkey motion -captured markers, 1105 

following the human muscle anatomy (N. Taubert et al., 2021) .1106 

This resulted in a monkey head avatar able to display the prototypical , naturalistic neutral, lip -smacking, 1107 

SBT and threat expressions, as well as expressions morphed across two categories in steps of 25 % 1108 

intensity between each pair of prototypical expressions. Additionally, we tested scrambled -frame versions 1109 

of the prototypical expressions (lacking temporal continuity of the movement), dynamic point -light 1110 

expressions, which contained the same movement patterns as the prototypical naturalistic avatars, but 1111 

no texture (instead white dot s of 200 ms limited -lifetime distributed over the avatar surface mesh on a 1112 

black background), and less realistic render types of the monkey avatar, which had been used in a previous 1113 

study to test for an “uncanny valley” of aversion towards artificial agent s (Siebert et al., 2020) : an 1114 

unnatural wireframe head without texture; a textured, but grayscale avatar ; and a colored, but still furless 1115 

avatar. Furthermore, we tested the monkeys’ categorization of the human avat ar displaying monkey 1116 

expressions, the human avatar presenting human expressions (smiling/happy , gasping/fearful ,1117 

scowling/angry ) and the monkey avatar showing those human expressions. 1118
