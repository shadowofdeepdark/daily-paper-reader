Abstract 

Learning to read requires linking auditory and visual information, yet how the developing brain maps information across sensory modalities remains poorly understood. To shed light on this topic we employed functional MRI to investigate hemodynamic brain responses during spoken and written word or pseudoword recognition in 61 primary school children with different levels of reading experience. Audiovisual representational similarity of activation patterns in the inferior frontal gyrus, inferior parietal lobule, superior temporal gyrus, and temporo-occipital cortex, increased linearly with school grade and this effect was largest in the left posterior superior temporal gyrus. Our results suggest that learning to read is related to a progressively increasing similarity of auditory and visual word representations within canonical language areas.

Introduction 

Learning to read is a fundamental stage in formal education and forms the neurobiological basis for lifelong learning. During early reading instruction, children learn to map written letters onto spoken sound, a process known as grapheme-to-phoneme conversion (Share, 1995). When becoming literate, grapheme-to-phoneme conversion enables children to link between written word forms, their corresponding spoken forms, and meanings, a process known as orthographic mapping (Ehri, 2014). Both behavioral and neuroimaging metrics of linking visual and auditory information have been implicated in children with reading disabilities, also known as dyslexia (Blau et al., 2010; Blomert, 2011; Blomert & Willems, 2010; Fraga González et al., 2017; Golden & Zenhausern, 1983). These  fi ndings highlight the importance of understanding neural circuits linking visual and auditory language inputs in the developing brain. 

Converging evidence for auditory language reveals that the left superior temporal gyrus (STG), middle temporal gyrus, (MTG) and inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) involve multiple aspects of speech processing, such as phonological segmentation, through ventral and dorsal pathways, and develop from the prenatal period through approximately ten years of age (Skeide & Friederici, 2016; Vigneau et al., 2006). In line with these auditory language networks, a recent meta-analysis of reading language areas in literate adults revealed a left-lateralized cluster comprising 10,732 voxels mainly in the IFG and temporoparietal regions (Turker et al., 2025). This largest cluster corresponds to the dorsal auditory language network linking across the frontal and temporoparietal regions via white-matter tracts of arcuate fasciculus and superior longitudinal fasciculus (Friederici, 2011). Collectively, these anatomical overlaps across distinct sensory modalities indicate that learning to read recruits cortical organizations that are also involved with auditory language processing. Results 

Sixty-one children (ages 5-10 years), including  fi rst grade ( n = 18), second grade ( n = 19), and fourth grade ( n = 24), learning to read the alpha-syllabic Devanagari script in India underwent fMRI while performing a semantic judgement task with stimuli from four conditions: written words, written pseudowords, spoken words, and spoken pseudowords. Our nine ROI masks were selected from the Harvard–Oxford cortical atlas and transformed into the MNI pediatric template for ages 7.5-13.5 years (see Figure 1A ). To estimate multimodal representational similarity for each participant, we constructed RDM for each ROI, and averaged across all cells that correlated between distinct modalities for each condition, but excluded cells corresponding to within-run comparisons since many shared confounds, such as scanner drift, may increase similarity (see 

Figure 1B ). All subsequent statistical analyses were performed using Fisher’s z-transformed multimodal representational similarity. 

pSTG shows the highest multimodal similarity between written and spoken forms 

We  fi rst tested the hypothesis that pSTG is more likely to represent word or pseudoword forms similarly. A one-sample t-test against zero multimodal similarity revealed signi fi cant multimodal similarity in pSTG for both words ( t(60) = 9.76, pFDR < .05, d = 1.25) and pseudowords ( t(60) = 9.83, 

pFDR < .05, d = 1.25). Further, the largest t-values observed in pSTG among the nine prede fi ned ROIs remained the largest across all 48 cortical atlases in the left hemisphere (see Figure S1 ). There was a signi fi cant multimodal dissimilarity of semantics in pSTG ( t(59) = -3.16, pFDR < .05, d =-0.40). All of the other ROIs also demonstrated signi fi cant similarity against zero for both word and pseudoword condition, but for semantic condition we observed signi fi cant dissimilarity against zero in trIFG and pSTG (see Table 1 and Figure 2A ).

Multimodal similarity of canonical reading areas linearly increases as school grade 

To determine if the multimodal similarity increased as a result of school experience, we  fi t a linear regression model with school grade as the predictor and multimodal similarity as the dependent variable. We con fi rmed that multimodal similarity in several ROIs, including the opIFG, aSMG, pSMG, Angular, pSTG, and TOFC, increased linearly as a function of school grade for both words (0.06 < β < 0.08, 0.02 < SE < 0.03, 2.48 < t(59) < 3.43, pFDR < .05) and pseudowords (0.06 < β < 0.10, 0.02 < SE < 0.03, 2.13 < t(59) < 4.04, pFDR < .05). No signi fi cant linear association was observed for the semantic condition (see Figure 2B ). These results were further supported by a second linear regression model  fi t with the reaction time of semantic judgement task during scanning as the predictor and multimodal similarity as the dependent variable. This second model demonstrated that multimodal similarity in the aSTG showed a signi fi cant linear association with reaction time for both words ( β = -0.36, SE = 0.11, t(59) = -3.12, pFDR < .05) and pseudowords ( β = -0.35, SE =0.11, t(59) = -3.12, pFDR < .05). These  fi ndings suggest that the linearly increased multimodal similarity observed across several ROIs was more likely driven by reading experience in school, rather than by attentional modulation. Further, all six ROIs that showed a linear increase in multimodal similarity as a function of school grade remained signi fi cant when the same regression analysis for the word condition was extended to all left-hemisphere cortical regions in the atlas ( n = 48). After excluding regions that also showed a signi fi cant effect of reaction time, we identi fi ed an additional ten cortical regions exhibiting linear increases in multimodal similarity with school grade (see Figure 3 and Figure S2 ).

Discussion 

Linking between written and spoken forms of language in the developing brain is a key question. In this study, we investigated how the developing brain represents written and spoken forms into reading circuitry using fMRI data from a large group of school children (ages 5-10 years) learning to read alpha-syllabic Devanagari script. By comparing hemodynamic brain responses across two sensory modalities with multivariate representational similarity analysis, we are able to estimate multimodal representational similarity in canonical reading regions. Here, we found both the region showing the highest multimodal similarly and its developmental modulation within the distributed reading regions. The pSTG exhibited the highest multimodal similarity for both words and pseudowords across modalities. In addition, multimodal similarity in the opIFG, aSMG, pSMG, angular gyrus, pSTG, and TOFC increased linearly with school grade. Taken together, these 

fi ndings suggest that the progressive increase in multimodal similarity within established reading regions likely re fl ects one of the critical neurodevelopmental changes associated with reading acquisition through formal schooling. 

The left pSTG is a prime candidate for higher multimodal representational similarity relative to other regions, since this region is known to underlie both reading and letter-sound integration in literate individuals (Gao et al., 2024; Turker et al., 2025). Our results are consistent with this account, showing that multimodal representations of words and pseudowords were most similar in the pSTG among canonical reading regions, and this effect persisted even across all atlas-de fi ned cortical regions in the left hemisphere. Comparable effects in the pseudoword condition are not surprising, given that pseudowords still consisted of authentic orthographic symbols associated with speech sounds, despite lacking familiar lexical combinations. Further, the signi fi cant negative multimodal similarity observed in the semantic condition within pSTG suggests that this region may be more strongly involved in the integration of sensory and

It is important to note that the greatest multimodal similarity in pSTG does not necessarily imply that its similarity was signi fi cantly greater than that of other canonical reading regions. Although examining the distinct functional roles of multimodal integration across reading regions was beyond the scope of the present study, future work will be necessary to determine whether the observed multimodal representational similarity speci fi cally re fl ects multimodal integration processes, rather than shared noise or other cognitive operations common to both word and pseudoword conditions. In addition, by replicating the established  fi nding of pSTG, we further support the robustness of the estimated multimodal representational similarity derived from the RSA framework using a cross-run approach that excluded within-run comparisons. 

The second central aim of this study was to address the gap between extensive evidence for universal reading regions in literate individuals and the comparatively limited understanding of the neurodevelopmental processes underlying the emergence of these regions. By focusing on the underexplored relationship between written and spoken language representations within canonical reading areas, we demonstrated that multimodal representational similarity progressively increased with school grade. Speci fi cally, multimodal similarity in the opIFG, aSMG, pSMG, angular gyrus, pSTG, and TOFC increased as a function of school grade. These  fi ndings are consistent with prior work showing that the degree of coactivation across written and spoken modalities in the IFG, inferior parietal lobule, STG, and fusiform gyrus predicts later reading performance (Preston et al., 2016). A critical limitation of these results, however, is that we cannot fully exclude the in fl uence of attentional engagement. For example, the greater multimodal similarity observed in fourth-grade children may partly re fl ect increased task engagement or sustained attention during the word and pseudoword conditions relative to younger children. This possibility is particularly relevant since the TOFC, particularly the visual word form area (VWFA), which is known to exhibit functional connectivity with the dorsal frontoparietal attention network (Chen et al., 2019). Although we attempted to partially account for attentional modulation by modeling reaction time, these analyses cannot fully rule out attentional contributions to the observed developmental effects. Therefore, we focused our interpretations on well-established canonical reading regions rather than all cortical areas, some of which may instead re fl ect the maturation of more general cognitive processes. 

Nevertheless, our  fi ndings provide new insight into previous reports of anatomical overlap between reading and speech circuities by demonstrating increased representational similarity across written and spoken modalities (Rueckl et al., 2015; Turker et al., 2025). Although the precise functional role of increased multimodal similarity within each reading-related region remains unclear, the present results provide preliminary neurodevelopmental evidence from children learning the alpha-syllabic Devanagari script, extending prior  fi ndings beyond alphabetic writing systems (Preston et al., 2016). While these interpretations remain to be veri fi ed in a longitudinal fMRI experiment, our  fi ndings offer a plausible account of the neurodevelopmental changes supporting the emergence of reading circuitry. An alternative explanation is that the observed increased multimodal similarity re fl ects differences in attention, working memory, or head motion across grades. Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was not a longitudinal experiment, although the sample size was relatively large for children aged 5-10 years. The cross-sectional design required analyzing data in a standard pediatric template space (“MNI Pediatric Asymmetrical template for ages 7.5–13.5 years”) rather than each child’s native space, potentially reducing sensitivity to individual variability in brain anatomy and introducing alignment-related noise that could obscure precise parcellation of our ROIs. Moreover, these ROIs were originally de fi ned in the literate adult brain. Because the cortical development occurs in the course of learning to read (Phan et al., 2021), future studies would ideally require tracking each child longitudinally from 1st to 4th grade with at least three or four scanning sessions per child. Second, a more comprehensive assessment of language skills closely related to reading outside the scanner would have strengthened this study. Because all thirty-six stimulus sets belonged to a single semantic category, our semantic judgement score did not directly re fl ect reading ability, limited to categorizing whether the stimulus is familiar or not. In contrast, the prior neurodevelopmental studies implemented standardized reading assessments, such as the Woodcock-Johnson III Tests of Achievement (WJ-III-ACH), outside the scanner (McNorgan et al., 2014; Preston et al., 2016). Consequently, our study design did not allow us to directly link reading pro fi ciency with the estimated multimodal representational similarity, limited to investigating the effect of school grade. Finally, several constraints in the fMRI experimental design should be considered. To accommodate the limited attention span of this age group, we employed a rapid event-related design with short stimulus presentations (3 s) and baseline intervals (4 s), and run durations of less than 5 minutes. Conclusion 

Considering the results outlined above, we identi fi ed both the cortical region exhibiting the most similar brain representations across modalities and the developmental changes of this similarity within the reading network. Speci fi cally, the pSTG showed the greatest multimodal similarity for both words and pseudowords, while multimodal similarity in the opIFG, aSMG, pSMG, angular gyrus, pSTG, and TOFC increased linearly with school grade. Collectively, these results indicate that increasing multimodal similarity within established reading regions may constitute a key neurodevelopmental process associated with reading acquisition during formal schooling. Owing to methodological challenges and the high cost of pediatric longitudinal fMRI studies, larger datasets remain scarce. The exploration of the developmental mechanisms underlying multimodal representational similarity is still in its beginnings. Nevertheless, our  fi rst study offers a plausible methodology and future hypothesis, which can be re fi ned as more extensive longitudinal data become available.

Methods 

Participants. We recruited a total of 93 children from three grade levels in New Delhi, India: 1st grade ( n = 30), 2nd grade ( n = 27) and 4th grade ( n = 35). All participants met the following inclusion criteria, con fi rmed by parental reports: (1) no contraindications for MRI scanning (e.g., absence of metallic implants or relevant medication), (2) no hearing or vision impairments, (3) no diagnosed language or developmental disorders. From the initial 93 participants, 32 were excluded due to excessive head motion, de fi ned as framewise displacement exceeding 2.4 mm in more than 10% of fMRI volumes, or insu ffi cient task engagement, de fi ned as a response rate below 50% within a run. The  fi nal dataset therefore included 61 children with three to eight usable runs: 1st grade ( n = 18), 2nd grade ( n = 19) and 4th grade ( n = 24). 

MRI data acquisition. MRI data were acquired on a GE SIGMA Architect 3T scanner with a 48 channel head coil. T1-weighted MPRAGE images were recorded with the parameters TE = 3.12 ms , TR = 2100 ms, TI = 900 ms,  fl ip angle = 8°,  fi eld of view = 256 × 256 mm, matrix size = 256 × 256 voxels, slice thickness = 1 mm, no gap between slices, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm, slice orientation = axial, and phase encoding direction = right-left. T2*-weighted EPI images (130 fMRI volumes) were recorded with the parameters TE = 30 ms, TR = 2000 ms,  fl ip angle = 90°, number of volumes = 130,  fi eld of view = 192 × 192 mm, in plane matrix size = 64 × 64 voxels, slice thickness = 3 mm, inter-slice gap = 0.2 mm, voxel size = 3 × 3 × 3 mm, slice orientation = axial, phase encoding direction = anterior-posterior, slice order = interleaved ascending, and multiband acceleration factor = 2.

MRI data preprocessing. Preprocessing was performed using fMRIPrep 25.1.4, which is based on 

Nipype 1.10.0. All the detailed process of preprocessing was automatically generated by fMRIPrep (see the Supplementary Methods ). 

Experimental paradigm. During fMRI scanning, participants completed an event-related task consisting of short stimuli from four conditions: written words, written pseudowords, spoken words, and spoken pseudowords. All words were masculine nouns drawn from the animal category and contained one or two syllables and three to six phonemes. Pseudowords were generated by substituting the initial consonant and vowel of each word with phonemes sharing the same articulatory place. Visual stimuli were presented in the middle of the screen in black font on a white background. The screen was positioned behind the scanner bore and viewed through a mirror mounted on the head coil. Auditory stimuli were recorded by a male native Hindi speaker. Each of the eight runs included 36 randomized events across the four conditions. Each stimulus was presented for 3 seconds. During spoken conditions, a black  fi xation cross was displayed at the center of the screen. The total experimental duration was approximately 4 minutes and 30 seconds. To maintain attention, participants performed a semantic judgement task, responding whether each stimulus represented an animal or not, using a MR-compatible button box. The experiment was programmed and presented using PsychoPy (v2021; Peirce et al., 2019). 

Subject-level fMRI analysis. We conducted a general linear model (GLM) implemented in Nilearn (v0.12.1) for Python (v3.12.12). Separately for each participant and run, fMRI data in pediatric space were scaled to percent signal change and spatially smoothed using a Gaussian kernel with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 9 mm. Multimodal representational similarity estimation. We estimated the multimodal representational similarity based on the widely established framework of representational similarity analysis (Kriegeskorte et al., 2008). For each participant and ROI, β-maps were extracted from all available runs and conditions, followed with vectorization across voxels. Pairwise RDM were computed using Pearson’s correlation distance (1 − r) across all run-wise activation patterns. Multimodal similarity metrics were calculated by averaging dissimilarities between written and spoken conditions across runs. Comparisons within the same run were excluded to minimize shared noise confounds. Separate multimodal similarity measures were subsequently computed for the word, pseudoword, and semantic conditions. 

Statistical analysis. All analyses reported in this study were conducted using two-sided tests and Fisher’s z-transformed multimodal representational similarity values. Outliers were identi fi ed and excluded using the interquartile range (IQR) method, such that values falling below the lower bound (de fi ned as 1.5 times the IQR below the  fi rst quartile) or above the upper bound (de fi ned as 1.5 times the IQR above the third quartile) were removed prior to statistical testing. To correct for multiple comparisons across ROIs, p-values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure (Benjamini–Hochberg method), with a signi fi cance threshold of q < 0.05. For each condition and ROI, we conducted one-sample t-tests to assess whether multimodal similarity signi fi cantly differed from zero, quantifying the magnitude and direction of deviation from zero similarity. Effect sizes were additionally reported using Cohen’s d. In addition, to examine whether multimodal similarity changes as a function of school grade, we  fi tted separate ordinary least squares linear regression models for each ROI and condition.
