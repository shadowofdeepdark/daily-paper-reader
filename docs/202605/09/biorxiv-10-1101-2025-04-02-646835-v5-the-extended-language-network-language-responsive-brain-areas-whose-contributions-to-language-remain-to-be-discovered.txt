# Abstract 37 

Although language neuroscience has largely focused on ‘core’ left frontal and temporal brain 38 

areas and their right-hemisphere homotopes, numerous other areas—cortical and subcortical— 39 

have been implicated in linguistic processing. However, these areas’ contributions to language 40 

remain unclear given that the evidence for their recruitment comes from diverse paradigms, 41 

many of which conflate language processing with perceptual, motor, or task-related cognitive 42 

processes. Using fMRI data from 772 participants (438 females, 334 males) performing an 43 

extensively validated language ‘localizer’ paradigm that isolates language processing from 44 

other processes, we a) delineate a comprehensive set of areas that respond reliably to language 45 

across written and auditory modalities, and b) evaluate these areas’ selectivity for language 46 

relative to a demanding non-linguistic task. In line with prior claims, many areas outside the 47 

core fronto-temporal network respond during language processing, and most of them show 48 

selectivity for language relative to general task demands. These language-selective areas of the 49 

extended language network include areas around the temporal poles, in the medial frontal 50 

cortex, in the hippocampus, and in the cerebellum, among others. Although distributed across 51 

many parts of the brain, the extended language-selective network still only comprises a small 52 

fraction (<5%) of the grey matter volume, challenging the view that the entire brain processes 53 

language. These newly identified language-selective areas can now be systematically 54 

characterized to decipher their contributions to language processing, including testing whether 55 

these contributions differ from those of the core language areas. 56 

# Significance statement 57 

Language processing consistently recruits a left-lateralized fronto-temporal brain network, but 58 

language tasks often additionally engage areas outside this core system. In an fMRI dataset of 59 

772 participants performing a validated language localizer task, we identified 17 brain areas 60 

outside the core fronto-temporal network that respond to both auditory and written language. 61 

Most of these areas show selectivity for language, including regions in the temporal poles, 62 

medial frontal cortex, hippocampus, and cerebellum. Despite its large number of components, 63 

this extended language network still only takes up a small fraction of the grey matter volume, 64 

challenging the view that the entire brain processes language. These findings lay the foundation 65 

for systematic characterization of these newly identified non-canonical language areas. 66

# Introduction 68 

Language processing engages a left-lateralized fronto-temporal brain network—the "language 69 

network"—which selectively supports comprehension and production across modalities, 70 

including computations related to word retrieval and combinatorial syntactic and semantic 71 

processing (Vigneau et al., 2006; Turker et al., 2023; Fedorenko et al., 2024). These areas have 72 

been consistently identified across diverse neuroimaging approaches (PET, fMRI, MEG, and 73 

intracranial recordings) and paradigms, and also implicated in studies of patients with aphasia 74 

(see Table OSF1 for select reviews, meta-analyses, and empirical papers). In addition to this 75 

‘core’ network and its right-hemispheric homotopic regions (Lindell, 2006; Ferstl et al., 2008; 76 

Mahowald & Fedorenko, 2016; Martin et al., 2022), several other brain areas have been 77 

implicated in aspects of language. In the cortex, these include medial frontal areas (Ardila, 78 

2020; Bohsali & Crosson, 2016; Hertrich et al., 2016), medial and lateral parietal areas (Hasson 79 

et al., 2007; Peschke et al., 2012; Papagno et al., 2017), temporal poles (Ferstl et al., 2008; 80 

Holland & Lambon Ralph, 2010; Lambon Ralph et al., 2017), ventral temporal areas (Krauss 81 

et al., 1996; Bédos Ulvin et al., 2017; Snyder et al., 2023), and even occipital areas in the blind 82 

(Bedny et al., 2011; Reich et al., 2011; Kim et al., 2017) but also sighted populations (Dikker 83 

et al., 2009, 2010; Seydell-Greenwald et al., 2023). Subcortically, language-implicated areas 84 

include the hippocampus (Covington & Duff, 2016; Dijksterhuis et al., 2024; reviews: Duff & 85 

Brown-Schmidt, 2012, 2017; Piai et al., 2016), the thalamus (Wahl et al., 2008; review: 86 

Klostermann & Ehlen, 2013), and basal ganglia structures, including the caudate, the pallidum, 87 

and the putamen (Booth et al., 2007; Crosson et al., 2003; Kotz, 2009; Oberhuber et al., 2013; 88 

Thibault et al., 2021, 2025). Finally, parts of the cerebellum have long been implicated in 89 

speech and language (reviews: Murdoch, 2010; Mariën et al., 2014; LeBel & D’Mello, 2023). 90 

However, the precise contributions of these non-canonical brain areas to language remain 91 

unclear. Much of the evidence comes from studies relying on single experimental paradigms, 92 

which often fail to isolate language processing from perceptual, motor, or general cognitive 93 

demands (for discussion, see Fedorenko et al., 2024). Further, the group-averaging approach— 94 

adopted by the majority of past neuroimaging studies—complicates across-study comparisons. 95 

In this approach, individual maps are averaged voxel-wise, and group-level activation peaks 96 

are reported in a standardized coordinate system (e.g., MNI; Mazziotta et al., 2001). However, 97 

inter-individual neuroanatomical variability leads to differences in structural and functional 98 

topographies (Frost & Goebel, 2012; Tahmasebi et al., 2012; Fedorenko, 2021; Michon et al., 99 

2022), which may shift the group peaks across studies even for the same paradigm. Besides, 100 

any macrocoanatomical structure encompasses multiple functionally distinct areas, making it 101 

difficult to unambiguously determine whether nearby peaks reported in different studies 102 

correspond to the same functional area (earlier discussions: Brett et al., 2002; Saxe et al., 2006). 103 

One approach that circumvents these challenges is functional localization (Saxe et al., 2006; 104 

Kanwisher, 2010; Fedorenko et al., 2010). Instead of averaging brains together, this approach 105 

identifies functional areas within individuals using ‘localizer’ tasks—robust contrasts that 106 

target a particular perceptual or cognitive process. This approach ensures that functionally 107 

equivalent area(s) are referred to across individuals and studies, supporting the systematic 108

characterization of each area’s responses across paradigms, as needed to understand its 109 

computations. At the same time, identifying these areas and testing their properties in each 110 

brain individually—effectively, working in each individual’s functional native space 111 

(regardless of the anatomical, stereotactic space that the data are registered to)—allows to 112 

preserve high functional resolution (Nieto-Castañón & Fedorenko, 2012): i.e., the ability to 113 

differentiate the relevant areas from nearby, functionally distinct areas. 114 

Here, we use an established language localizer and a large fMRI dataset to search for language-115 

selective areas outside the core language network. In line with past claims, we find several such 116 

areas, and many of them show selectivity for language relative to a demanding non-linguistic 117 

task, which makes them exciting targets for future investigations. 118 

# Methods 119 

# Overview 120 

We leverage a dataset of 772 participants each of whom performed an extensively validated 121 

reading-based language localizer (Fedorenko et al., 2010) and one or two additional tasks 122 

(Figure 1A ). In particular, 489 of the 772 participants performed a demanding spatial working 123 

memory task, which allowed us to begin evaluating the selectivity of the non-canonical 124 

language-responsive areas. 86 of these 489 participants additionally performed an auditory 125 

version of the language localizer (Malik-Moraleda, Ayyash et al., 2022), which allowed us to 126 

evaluate whether the observed language responses are robust to modality (reading vs. 127 

listening). If a brain region, akin to the core frontal and temporal language areas, responds to 128 

both written and auditory linguistic inputs (Fedorenko et al., 2010; Vagharchakian et al., 2012; 129 

Regev et al., 2013; Deniz et al., 2019), its computations plausibly have to do with the content 130 

of the linguistic signals rather than their surface (visual or auditory) properties. 131 

In the critical analysis, we define regions of interest functionally within individual participants. 132 

To constrain the selection of these individual-level functional regions of interest (fROIs), we 133 

use functional parcels derived from a group-level representation of the language localizer 134 

activity (Fedorenko et al., 2010). Because the fMRI signal is weaker in subcortical areas, which 135 

are further away from the receiver coil, and to ensure that we don’t miss any language-136 

responsive areas, we complement these parcels with subcortical anatomical parcels, which we 137 

use in the same way (to constrain individual-level fROIs). Finally, because standard cortical 138 

atlases are commonly used for ROI definition, we comprehensively examine language 139 

responsiveness and selectivity in three such atlases, in order to specify the relevant subsets of 140 

areas within which reliable and selective responses to language can be observed, and to further 141 

highlight the importance of functional localization within individuals instead of using group-142 

level anatomical masks. 143

# Tasks 163 

## Reading-based language localizer task 164 

Participants passively read English sentences and lists of pronounceable nonwords, which 165 

appeared on the screen one word/nonword at a time ( Figure 1B, top ). Each trial started with a 166 

100ms fixation, followed by a 12-element-long sequence of words/nonwords presented at the 167 

rate of 450ms. At the end of each trial, a drawing of a hand appeared for 400ms, and participants 168 

had to press a button every time they saw this drawing. The button press task was included to 169 

help the participants stay alert and focused during the task. Each trial ended with a blank screen 170 

presented for 100ms. The task followed a blocked design. The total duration of each trial was 171 

6 seconds, and 3 trials were included in each block. Each run included 16 18-second blocks (8 172 

per condition). Additionally, 5 fixation blocks of 14 seconds were included in each run, for a 173 

total run duration of 358 seconds. Each participant completed two runs. The Sentences >174 

Nonwords contrast targets brain areas that support language processing (including mental 175 

operations related to word retrieval, syntactic structure building, and semantic composition), 176 

while excluding perceptual processing (see Figure OSF1 —available at https://osf.io/7594t/— 177 

for evidence that this contrast includes areas sensitive to sub-lexical/phonological processing; 178 

also: Bozic et al., 2015; Regev et al., 2022). 179 

It is important to note that although this particular version of the language localizer uses written 180 

stimuli, the core frontal and temporal areas activated by the written Sentences>Nonwords 181 

contrast overlap almost perfectly with areas identified with auditory contrasts between 182 

language processing and a perceptually similar condition, such as backwards speech or 183 

acoustically degraded speech (e.g., Fedorenko et al., 2010; Scott et al., 2017; Malik-Moraleda, 184 

Ayyash et al., 2022). Moreover, the network of areas that emerges for the Sentences>Nonwords 185 

contrast (and similar contrasts) closely matches one of the intrinsic brain networks recoverable 186 

from individual patterns of functional connectivity, as measured during naturalistic conditions 187

## Auditory language localizer task 190 

Participants passively listened to intact and acoustically degraded passages from Alice in 191 

Wonderland (Carroll, 1865; Figure 1B, middle ). The degraded versions of each passage were 192 

created using a procedure introduced in Scott et al. (2017): intact passages were low-pass 193 

filtered (passband 500 Hz) and mixed with a time-varying noise track derived from the same 194 

clip (generated by randomizing consecutive 0.02-s segments, amplitude-modulated by the 195 

intact signal, and low-pass filtered to attenuate very high frequencies). The noise level was 196 

adjusted until the resulting clips were unintelligible. Although the degraded clips largely 197 

preserve prosodic patterns, the key computations carried out by the language areas (related to 198 

word recognition, syntactic structure building, and semantic composition; e.g., Shain, Kean et 199 

al., 2024) cannot be performed because the content is not comprehensible, leading to a much 200 

weaker response compared to the response to the intact clips. 201 

Each trial consisted of an 18-second recording. Each run included 12 18-second trials (4 per 202 

condition; in addition to the intact and degraded conditions, the experiment included a third 203 

condition not included in any analyses here—recordings in an unfamiliar foreign language). 204 

Additionally, 3 fixation blocks of 12 seconds were included in each run, for a total run duration 205 

of 252 seconds. Each participant completed three runs. Similar to the reading-based localizer, 206 

the Intact > Degraded contrast targets brain areas that support language processing. 207 

## A demanding non-linguistic (spatial working memory) task 208 

Participants performed a spatial working memory task consisting of easier and harder trials 209 

(e.g., Fedorenko et al., 2013; Assem et al., 2020). Each trial started with a 500ms fixation. 210 

Then, a 3 x 4 grid appeared in the center of the screen, and a series of locations were flashed 211 

in blue at the rate of 1 second per flash ( Figure 1B, bottom ). In the easy condition, one location 212 

flashed at a time (four locations in total); in the hard condition, two locations flashed at a time 213 

(eight in total). Participants were instructed to keep track of the locations. Each trial ended with 214 

a two-choice forced choice task: two grids with different sets of locations were shown side by 215 

side, and participants had to choose the grid that shows the locations just presented. They were 216 

told whether they chose correctly (green checkmark) or not (red cross). The task followed a 217 

blocked design. The total duration of each trial was 8 seconds, and 4 trials were included in 218 

each block. Each run included 12 32-second blocks (6 per condition). Additionally, 4 fixation 219 

blocks of 16 seconds were included in each run, for a total run duration of 448 seconds. Each 220 

participant completed 2 runs. The Hard > Easy contrast targets brain areas that support 221 

demanding cognitive tasks, including mental operations associated with working memory, 222 

attention, and cognitive control. 223

# fMRI data acquisition 231 

Structural and functional data collected in the Athinoula A. Martinos Imaging Center at the 232 

McGovern Institute for Brain Research at MIT. Structural and functional data were acquired 233 

using a Siemens Trio 3 Tesla scanner before or after a Prisma upgrade (n=508 and n=264, 234 

respectively) and a 32-channel head coil. High-resolution, whole-brain anatomical images were 235

angle = 9°). Functional T2*-weighted images were acquired using three different whole-brain 239

30 ms; flip angle = 90°; matrix size 96 x 96 mm; MB acceleration factor 0; Sequence 2, 242 

Siemens Prisma : n = 73, 52 near-axial slices, 2 × 2 × 2 mm isotropic voxels; TR = 2,000 ms; 243

ms; TE = 30 ms; flip angle = 90°; matrix size 104 x 104 mm; MB acceleration factor 3). The 246 

first 10 s of each run were excluded to allow for steady state magnetization. 247 

# fMRI data preprocessing 248 

fMRI data were analyzed using SPM12 (release 7487), CONN EvLab module (release 19b), 249 

and other custom MATLAB scripts. Each participant’s functional and structural data were 250 

converted from DICOM to NIFTI format. All functional scans were coregistered and resampled 251 

using 4 th degree B-spline interpolation to the first scan of the first session (Friston et al., 1995). 252 

Potential outlier scans were identified from the resulting subject-motion estimates as well as 253 

from BOLD signal indicators using default thresholds in CONN preprocessing pipeline (5 254 

standard deviations above the mean in global BOLD signal change, or framewise displacement 255 

values above 0.9 mm; Nieto-Castañón, 2020). Functional and structural data were 256 

independently normalized into a common space (the Montreal Neurological Institute [MNI] 257 

template; IXI549Space) using SPM12 unified segmentation and normalization procedure 258 

(Ashburner & Friston, 2005) with a reference functional image computed as the mean 259 

functional data after realignment across all timepoints omitting outlier scans. The output data 260 

were resampled to a common bounding box between MNI-space coordinates (-90, -126, -72) 261 

and (90, 90, 108), using 2mm isotropic voxels and 4 th degree B-spline interpolation for the 262 

functional data, and 1mm isotropic voxels and trilinear interpolation for the structural data. 263 

Last, the functional data were smoothed spatially using spatial convolution with a 4 mm 264 

FWHM Gaussian kernel (following a reviewer’s request, we additionally provide unsmoothed 265 

activation maps on OSF (https://osf.io/7594t/); see also Figure OSF2 for evidence that 266 

individual-level activation maps are highly similar regardless of the preprocessing approach: 267 

with vs. without normalization to the common space, and between our volumetric SPM-based 268 

pipeline and a surface-based Freesurfer pipeline). 269

# fMRI data first-level modeling 270 

Responses in individual voxels were estimated using a General Linear Model (GLM) in which 271 

each experimental condition was modeled with a boxcar function convolved with the canonical 272 

hemodynamic response function (HRF) (fixation was modeled implicitly). Temporal 273 

autocorrelations in the BOLD signal timeseries were accounted for by a combination of high-274 

pass filtering with a 128 seconds cutoff, and whitening using an AR(0.2) model (first-order 275 

autoregressive model linearized around the coefficient a=0.2) to approximate the observed 276 

covariance of the functional data in the context of Restricted Maximum Likelihood estimation 277 

(ReML). In addition to experimental condition effects, the GLM design included first-order 278 

temporal derivatives for each condition (included to model variability in the HRF delays), as 279 

well as nuisance regressors to control for the effect of slow linear drifts, subject-specific motion 280 

parameters (6 parameters), and potential outlier scans (identified during preprocessing as 281 

described above) on the BOLD signal. 282 

# Group-level parcels used to constrain the definition of individual 283 

# fROIs 284 

The group-level functional parcels were created using the group-constrained subject-specific 285 

approach (GSS; Fedorenko et al., 2010) as implemented in the spm_ss toolbox (available for 286 

download from: https://www.evlab.mit.edu/resources). This approach helps determine which 287 

parts of the activation landscape are consistent across participants and establish 288 

correspondences across individuals. To do so, thresholded binarized individual maps for a 289 

particular contrast of interest are overlaid in a common brain space to create a probabilistic 290 

activation overlap map (or ‘atlas’). Subsequently, this map is divided into parcels using a 291 

watershed image parcellation algorithm (see Julian et al., 2012 for the application of this 292 

approach to the ventral visual areas). 293 

Here, we used a set of 706 individual maps (the subset of the native English speakers; Figure 294 

1A ) and selected the 20% most language-responsive voxels across the brain in each individual. 295 

This relatively liberal individual-level threshold increases the likelihood of capturing all 296 

language-responsive areas, including those with weaker and less spatially consistent responses. 297 

It is worth noting that the average t-value across participants for these voxels was still quite 298 

high: 2.02 (SD=0.6), with the average minimum t-value of 0.92, and the average maximum t-299 

value of 14.17). In the resulting overlap map, the value of each voxel, divided by the total 300 

number of participants, represents the proportion of participants for whom that voxel belongs 301 

to the top 20% of most language-responsive voxels (the highest value in this map is ~0.78, in 302 

line with Lipkin et al., 2022). The overlap map was thresholded to remove voxels with values 303 

below 0.1 (i.e., voxels present in fewer than 10%, or 71 of the 706 participants) and smoothed 304 

with a 6 mm full-width half-maximum Gaussian kernel, to avoid over-parcellation. Finally, an 305 

image parcellation (watershed) algorithm was run to identify the main “hills” in the activation 306 

landscape (Meyer, 1991). 307 

In our dataset, this algorithm identified 27 parcels (of size 150 voxels or larger): 11 in the left 308 

hemisphere, 8 in the right hemisphere, 3 midline bilateral parcels, and 5 parcels in the 309

# Estimation of response to the three tasks in the individual language 319 

# fROIs 320 

Within each parcel, we defined a subject-specific functional ROI (fROI) by selecting the 10% 321 

of voxels showing the strongest response to the language localizer contrast ( Sentences > 322 

Nonwords ) (see Figures 2 and 3 for sample fROIs; see Figure OSF4 for evidence that the 323 

results are near-identical if the fROIs are defined as voxels that pass a fixed whole-brain 324 

significance threshold of p<.001). Subsequently, we estimated the responses in these fROIs to 325 

the conditions of the auditory language localizer ( Intact speech and Degraded speech ) and the 326 

spatial working memory task ( Hard spatial WM and Easy spatial WM ). To estimate the 327 

responses to the Sentences and Nonwords conditions, we relied on an across-runs cross-328 

validation procedure, which ensures that different subsets of the data are used for fROI 329 

definition and response estimation (e.g., Kriegeskorte et al., 2010). 330 

# Statistical analyses 331 

The data were analyzed using linear mixed-effect models as implemented in the lmerTest 332 

package in R (Kuznetsova et al., 2017). We ran a separate analysis for each fROI comparing 333 

the critical and baseline conditions in each task: the reading-based language localizer, the 334 

auditory language localizer, and the spatial working memory task using the following formula: 335 

EffectSize ~ 1 + Condition * Task + (1 | Participant) 336 

The categorical predictor of Condition was deviation-coded using the treatment contrast which 337 

specified the control condition of each task as the baseline (the reading-based language 338 

localizer: Sentences = 1, Nonwords = 0; the auditory language localizer: Intact speech = 1, 339 

Degraded speech = 0; the spatial WM task: Hard WM = 1, Easy WM = 0). The categorical 340 

predictor of Task was deviation-coded using the treatment contrast and specifying the reading 341 

language localizer as the baseline. 342 

Subsequently, we ran three post-hoc pairwise comparisons to test (i) differences between the 343 

critical and control conditions for each task (i.e., Sentences > Nonwords , Intact speech >344 

Degraded speech , and Hard WM > Easy WM ); (ii) whether in each task the responses to the 345 

critical and control conditions significantly differed from 0 (e.g., Sentences > 0 and Nonwords 346 

> 0 in the reading language task); and (iii) whether the responses to the critical condition 347 

significantly differed between a) each of the two language conditions (i.e., Sentences > Intact 348

speech ) and b) the non-linguistic Hard WM condition (i.e., Sentences > Hard WM , and Intact 349 

speech > Hard WM ). We do not apply a correction for multiple comparisons (regions) here 350 

because we are asking an independent question for each region (rather than asking whether the 351 

effect holds in any of the regions). Besides, the approach already has internal replicability 352 

“checks” built into it, including an across-runs cross-validation procedure for the reading-based 353 

contrast and generalization to the auditory language contrast. That said, most of the critical 354 

effects would survive a correction for the number of regions given the size of our sample (all 355 

GSS and subcortical regions and majority of regions evaluated within standard atlases). 356 

It is important to note that although different subsets of participants completed the three tasks 357 

(all n=772 completed the reading-based language localizer; n=489 of the 772 additionally 358 

completed the spatial WM task; and n=86 of the 489 additionally completed the auditory 359 

language localizer; Figure 1A ), linear mixed-effects models are well-suited to handle such data 360 

imbalances (Little & Rubin, 2019). In particular, this approach efficiently estimates the effects 361 

of interest while controlling for data sparsity (assuming greater uncertainty where data are 362 

sparse by implementing partial pooling strategy, which “shrinks” the intercepts of participants 363 

with less data points towards the population mean more than those of participants who 364 

completed all tasks) and is robust under the assumption that the missing data are missing at 365 

random, as is reasonable here. In addition, this approach has the advantage of using all available 366 

data compared to analyzing each task separately (Snijders & Bosker, 2011; Brown, 2021). 367 

Nevertheless, to establish the statistical robustness of our results, we additionally report the 368 

results for the subset of the n=86 participants who completed all three tasks and thus where the 369 

effects could be estimated with a full within-participant design ( Figure OSF5 ). 370 

Finally, although the three functional sequences used in this dataset (see fMRI data acquisition) 371 

differ in some acquisition parameters which may be reflected in BOLD responses, we did not 372 

explicitly model sequence differences given that (1) all contrasts of interest are within-task, 373 

within-run comparisons between conditions, and all effect size estimates come from fROIs that 374 

are defined using functional data acquired with the same sequence as the data used for BOLD 375 

response estimation. Therefore, any sequence-specific biases signal acquisition would affect 376 

all conditions equally and thus cancel out; and (2) the mixed models include by-participant 377 

random intercepts, which should absorb any residual between-participant variability in overall 378 

signal intensity or SNR. 379 

# Estimation of the language system size 380 

To estimate the size of the language system, we first used individual-level t-maps to extract 381 

the number of voxels that pass a significance threshold of p<.001, uncorrected, for Sentences 382 

> Nonwords contrast within the union of all core and extended parcels. To convert this value 383 

into cubic centimeters, we multiplied the number of voxels by their size (i.e., 8mm 3, given 384 

that all functional data were resampled to the 2x2x2mm resolution). It is important to note 385 

that MNI template tends to be larger than a typical brain. For example, in our data the amount 386 

of grey matter in the MNI-normalized probability map was estimated to be, on average, 387 

942cm 3 (SD = 76cm 3, min = 548cm 3, max = 1112cm 3), whereas in the native space, it was, 388 

on average, 748cm 3 (SD = 74cm 3, min = 483cm 3, max = 998cm 3). As a result, the absolute 389

To estimate the proportion that the language system, including the cortical and cerebellar 392

cerebellar, subcortical, and brain stem), we used the anatomical segmentation images 394

data. For each participant, we estimated the grey matter amount as the sum of voxelwise grey 401 

matter probabilities multiplied by voxel volume. 402 

# Comparison with Standard Cortical Atlases 403 

To facilitate comparisons with other studies, where standard anatomical atlases are often used, 404 

we evaluated language responses in three cortical atlases: 1) the Desikan-Killiany-Tourville 405 

atlas (Klein & Tourville, 2012), 2) the Harvard-Oxford Cortical atlas (Desikan et al., 2006), 406 

and 3) the multi-modal Glasser atlas (Glasser et al., 2016) (see Figure S5A for information on 407 

the overlap between the functional and anatomical cortical parcels). The aim of these analyses 408 

is to evaluate the usefulness of standard atlases as group-constraints for subject-specific fROIs 409 

and not making any claims about the relationship of the functional language areas and 410 

macroanatomical landmarks. 411 

To create a volume-based DKT parcellation, we ran the cortical reconstruction (using the recon-412 

all Freesurfer standard pipeline) on the MNI template (IXI549Space), which was used in this 413 

study to normalize each participant’s data. The resulting parcels (the aparc.DKTatlas+aseg.mgz 414 

file) were converted to the NIFTI format and smoothed with a 2 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel. 415 

The smoothing step is necessary to slightly increase the spatial extent of the parcels (because 416 

they are aligned to the cortical surface, they are relatively thin) as well as accommodate 417 

potential small differences between the surface space and the volumetric space. The Harvard-418 

Oxford atlas is volume-based, so no transformation was needed. Finally, for the Glasser atlas, 419 

the MNI-based volumetric version was downloaded from 420 

https://figshare.com/articles/dataset/HCP-421 

MMP1_0_projected_on_MNI2009a_GM_volumetric_in_NIfTI_format/3501911 and resliced 422 

to the 2mm IXI549Space in SPM. 423 

For each atlas, we performed two analyses: an analysis where the whole parcel is used as the 424 

ROI (an identical set of voxels is used across participants), and an analysis where within each 425 

parcel, we define a subject-specific fROI, as we did in the main analysis (by selecting the 10% 426 

of voxels showing the strongest response to the language localizer contrast). Statistical analyses 427 

were performed on the responses extracted from these two kinds of ROIs (anatomical group-428 

level ROIs and individual fROIs) using the same approach of linear mixed-effects models, as 429 

described above. 430

# Results 436 

We considered a region to contain a fROI responsive to language if the following conditions 437 

were met: 1) the response to both of the language contrasts ( Sentences > Nonwords and Intact 438 

> Degraded speech ) is positive and significant (p<.05), and 2) the response to both of the 439 

critical conditions (i.e., Sentences for the reading language localizer and Intact speech for the 440 

auditory language localizer) falls significantly above the fixation baseline. We further 441 

considered a region to contain a fROI selective for language if, in addition to the 442 

abovementioned conditions, the critical language conditions ( Sentences or Intact speech )443 

elicited a reliably greater response than the critical condition for the non-linguistic task (i.e., 444 

Hard WM ). Note that here, we only evaluate selectivity for language relative to one task: a non-445 

linguistic working memory task. This approach allows us to determine whether a brain region 446 

responds to any cognitive task, or whether the response reflects processing required for 447 

language comprehension (through reading and listening) but not for performing the working 448 

memory task. This limited definition of selectivity already rules out some of the language-449 

responsive regions as supporting computations that are not specific to language (see Results), 450 

but it is of course possible that some of the regions found here to be selective will later be found 451 

to not be selective relative to other non-linguistic tasks (such as processing music; or processing 452 

non-verbal communicative signals; or processing non-linguistic meanings). 453 

# The extended set of language-selective regions 454 

Twenty-two of the 27 parcels identified by the whole-brain GSS analysis (Methods) contained 455 

fROIs that responded to language across modalities, with the exception of the three parcels in 456 

the occipital cortex, the parcel in the left precuneus / posterior ACC, and the parcel in the left 457 

parahippocampal gyrus (the fROIs in these parcels either did not show a significant response 458 

to one of the language contrasts or responded at or below baseline to one or both of the critical 459 

language conditions). Of the 22 language-responsive regions, 20 (including 5 core LH regions 460 

and their RH homotopic regions) showed selective responses to language with little or no 461 

response to the demanding non-linguistic task; both of the exceptions were located in the 462 

cerebellum ( Figures 2 and 3, Table 1 ; see Table S1 for the details of the statistical tests; for 463 

completeness, see Figure S1 for the profiles of the regions excluded due to small parcel size). 464 

Note that in addition to these language-selective areas, most of these parcels contain areas that 465 

respond strongly to the non-linguistic task and not to the language tasks (see Figure S3 ), in 466 

line with past evidence that distinct functional areas often lie near each other within the same 467 

anatomical structure (e.g., Fedorenko et al., 2012; Braga et al., 2020; Du et al., 2024)—a 468 

finding that reinforces the importance of functionally defining the relevant region within each 469 

individual. 470

# Language responses in three standard brain atlases 562 

To facilitate comparisons with other work, we comprehensively evaluated language responses 563 

in three cortical atlases that vary in the granularity of their parcellation. These analyses serve 564 

two goals. First, they help identify anatomical areas within which reliable and selective 565 

responses to language can be observed within individuals. And second, they highlight the 566 

critical importance of individual-level functional localization. 567 

When using the functional localization approach (i.e., selecting—for each individual 568 

separately—the top 10% of most active voxels based on their language localizer map), a 569 

number of areas emerge as containing language-selective fROIs ( Figure 5 ). Full functional 570 

profiles of the atlas parcels are presented on Figure S6 (see Table OSF2 for details of statistical 571 

tests in all regions in the three atlases). In the DKT atlas, 20 of the 72 parcels contain language-572 

selective fROIs: 13 in the left hemisphere and 7 in the right hemisphere. In the Harvard-Oxford 573 

Cortical atlas, 42 of the 96 parcels contain language-selective fROIs: 26 in the left hemisphere 574 

and 16 in the right hemisphere. Finally, in the Glasser multimodal atlas, 95 of the 380 parcels 575

# Discussion 645 

Many brain areas outside the left fronto-temporal network have been previously implicated in 646 

language processing ( Table OSF1 ). Here, we systematically searched for language-responsive 647 

areas across the brain. To do so, we a) used a paradigm that isolates language processing from 648 

perceptual, motor, and task-related cognitive demands (Fedorenko et al., 2010), b) included a 649 

large dataset (n=772 participants), c) examined language responses across modalities, and d) 650 

evaluated language selectivity relative to a demanding cognitive task. In addition to the core 651 

left hemisphere areas and their homotopes, we identified 17 areas (7 cortical and 10 subcortical, 652 

including 5 cerebellar) that respond to auditory and written language, with 14 of these areas 653 

showing selectivity for language relative to a demanding non-linguistic task. 654 

The contributions of non-canonical language areas to language and cognition. 655 

Establishing that a brain area responds to language across modalities and not during a non-656 

linguistic task ensures that its activity is not driven by perceptual or general cognitive demands. 657 

Still, the precise contributions of these language-responsive and language-selective areas 658 

remain to be discovered. Past studies may hold some clues, but relating our results to prior 659 

work is challenging: we cannot be certain that the areas we found here correspond to those 660 

identified in prior work, because macroanatomy is a poor guide to function in the association 661 

cortex (Frost & Goebel, 2012; Tahmasebi et al., 2012; also, Figure S5B ). Adopting the 662 

functional localization approach has led to several robust and replicable findings about the core 663 

language network, including its selectivity for language and its role in linguistic computations 664 

(see Fedorenko et al., 2024 for a review). The extended language areas we identified here 665 

constitute new targets for similar systematic investigations, which should i) evaluate the 666 

selectivity of these areas relative to other functions that bear similarities to language, ii) probe 667 

sensitivity of these areas to linguistic demands associated with lexical and combinatorial 668 

processing (in controlled and naturalistic paradigms), and iii) directly compare these areas’ 669 

contributions to those of the core language areas. 670 

The non-selective language-responsive areas may still meaningfully contribute to language 671 

processing. We have identified three such areas: two in the right cerebellum (Casto et al., 672 

2025a) and one in the left thalamus. Higher spatial resolution approaches may eventually reveal 673 

that distinct neural sub-populations create the appearance of mixed selectivity. However, if the 674

2013; Theofanopoulou & Boeckx, 2016; Wolpert et al., 1998). 678 

Activation during a “language task” may not always reflect linguistic processing. 679 

Some past studies have reported neural activity during language tasks in brain areas that do not 680 

show language-responsiveness in our study. However, activation during “language tasks” (i.e., 681 

tasks that use verbal materials) may not always reflect linguistic processing. Many language 682 

paradigms tax both linguistic and general cognitive demands, so a response in such paradigms 683 

could reflect the latter. For example, Thibault et al. (2021, 2025) have argued for overlap 684 

between syntactic processing and tool use in the basal ganglia. However, both the syntactic and 685 

tool-use tasks in those studies require cognitive processes beyond syntactic and tool-use-related 686 

computations. We show that the language paradigms that are not confounded with task 687 

demands do not consistently engage any basal ganglia structures ( Figure 4B ): whereas the 688 

reading language contrast elicits a small response in the left caudate and putamen, these 689 

responses do not generalize to the auditory language contrast, which rules out the possibility 690 

that these brain areas are engaged in language processing. These findings suggest that the 691 

syntactic complexity manipulation in Thibault et al.’s studies may be engaging these areas due 692 

to general cognitive demands and not by virtue of syntactic computations (see Figure S4 for 693 

evidence that basal ganglia areas respond strongly to task demands). 694 

Similarly, Seydell-Greenwald et al. (2023) report responses to auditory language tasks in the 695 

primary visual cortex (V1) of sighted individuals. We do not replicate this finding:, the weak 696 

response in occipital areas to the reading-based contrast (mostly due to greater deactivation 697 

during nonword reading) does not generalize to the auditory modality, which rules out the 698 

possibility that V1 implements linguistic computations (cf. evidence of V1 supporting language 699 

processing in blind individuals; Bedny et al., 2011; Lane et al., 2015; Pant et al., 2020; 700 

Czarnecka et al., 2025). The effects observed in Seydell-Greenwald et al. (2023) might be 701 

explained by the fact that their contrasts are confounded with general cognitive demands: one 702 

contrast entails listening to sentences and answering questions about them vs. listening to 703 

reversed speech and responding to a beep (see Ozernov-Palchik, O'Brien et al., in press for 704 

additional discussion of difficulty confounds in this paradigm), and the other contrast compares 705 

listening to words and pressing a button for catch trials (thus requiring attention to the task 706 

beyond the lexical processing demands) vs. a low-level baseline. Attentional modulation of V1 707 

is well-established (Somers et al., 1999), so the observed responses plausibly reflect attention-708 

related engagement. To argue that a brain area supports linguistic computations, one needs to 709 

ensure that it responds to language across tasks and modalities but also that the language task 710 

is not confounded by difficulty or attentional demands. Previous work has established that the 711 

core language areas are relatively insensitive to task demands, showing similarly strong 712 

responses during passive comprehension and paradigms that include tasks (Fedorenko et al., 713 

2010; Diachek, Blank, Siegelman et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2025). Here, we extend these findings 714 

to several non-canonical language areas but also question some past findings of “language” 715 

responses that have alternative explanations. 716

A small and well-defined subset of the brain implements language processing. 717 

Despite ample evidence for functional specialization in the brains of humans (Kanwisher, 718 

2010) and non-human animals (Tsao et al., 2006), some continue to argue against the idea of 719 

stable structure in the brain, emphasizing the distributed, dynamic, and interactive nature of 720 

cognitive processes, including lnguage (e.g., Pessoa, 2022; Forkel & Hagoort, 2024; Drijvers 721 

et al., 2025). Deep engagement with this debate is beyond the scope of this article, but two 722 

points are worth clarifying. First, the fact that many areas—sometimes in distant parts of the 723 

brain—are engaged by language comprehension does not imply that the “entire brain” supports 724 

this function. Although the extended language network spans almost every major component 725 

of the brain, within each component, language regions occupy a small fraction of brain tissue. 726 

Second, linguistic inputs can unquestionably engage many brain regions beyond those that 727 

specifically support language processing: vivid descriptions of faces or scenes can engage 728 

category-selective visual areas, a story about a misunderstanding can engage the Theory of 729 

Mind network, and a horror story can engage the amygdala (see Casto, et al., 2025b for 730 

discussion). However, all these brain regions can also be engaged by non-linguistic inputs. The 731 

ability of a brain region to be engaged by language does not make it a “language region” any 732 

more than its ability to be engaged by visual inputs makes it a “visual region”. Furthermore, 733 

the fact that the language network needs to interact with other brain areas does not undermine 734 

its functional distinctness from those areas and its special role in language processing. As long 735 

as different components within the language network interact more strongly with one another 736 

than with other networks—for which ample evidence exists (Blank et al., 2014; Braga et al., 737 

2020; Du et al., 2024, 2025; Shain & Fedorenko, 2025)—the language network and other 738 

cognitive networks can be treated as meaningfully distinct objects of study (Simon, 1962). 739 

Parcels derived from functional data have advantages over anatomical parcels for 740 

constraining individual fROIs. 741 

For completeness and ease of comparison with past studies, we explored the possibility of using 742 

standard anatomical atlases to constrain individual fROIs (rather than the parcels derived from 743 

a group-level representation of brain activity; Fedorenko et al., 2010; Julian et al., 2012). 744 

Examining individual activation maps (or maps derived from functional connectivity patterns: 745 

Braga et al., 2020; Du et al., 2024, 2025; Shain & Fedorenko, 2025) against standardized brain 746 

parcellations reveals two issues. First, individual topographies often do not align with the 747 

boundaries of the atlas areas: a contiguous functional region may get broken up by a boundary, 748 

or—for finer-grained atlases—may get assigned to different atlas areas across individuals 749 

because of inter-individual topographic variability (see Figure S5B for examples). For studies 750 

focusing on a particular functional network, we therefore recommend functional parcels over 751 

anatomical/multimodal atlases. Critically, however, whichever approach is chosen, the regions 752 

of analysis should be defined functionally within individuals (using localizers or functional-753 

connectivity-based parcellations) to avoid conflating nearby networks at the group level 754 

(Figure 4 ; see Figure S3 for evidence that most language parcels include regions of the 755 

Multiple Demand network). And second, using large, anatomical ROIs creates an illusion that 756 

language processing engages large chunks of the cortex, while in reality these extensive regions 757 

contain (sometimes multiple) small language-responsive regions (see Figures 2A and 3A for 758
